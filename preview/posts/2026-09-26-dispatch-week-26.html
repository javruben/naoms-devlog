Two things changed this week

Every kind of event now declares: vow or weather

Weather and vows sets one rule for anything built on NAOMS. A vow is signed, kept, and delivered to people who were away. Weather is held in memory for whoever is online right now, and is allowed to vanish. Weather has to pass four tests: it updates fast, nobody needs its history, missing it while offline is fine, and losing it on restart is fine.

The same article records two results. In one-to-one conversations a sender can now see who has read their message, and your own unread marker survives a restart. Weather delivered between two real devices has not been seen outside tests, so "memory only, sent to your other devices" is a design and a code path, not an observed behaviour.

A new chat opens empty, on purpose

Your first chat starts empty, on purpose covers the assistant built into NAOMS. It used to open with a scripted hello and three suggestion buttons. A new chat, the very first included, now shows the logo, one short greeting line and an empty box, and waits for you.

The greeting starts as a plain "Hello". The model behind the chat is then asked for a warmer line of at most six words, and if it gives nothing usable the plain "Hello" stays, so the screen never shows friendliness nobody wrote. The old welcome was deleted rather than switched off.

Where the week's landings clustered

This is landing-subject frequency: a read of the subjects of the week's first-parent landings onto main, grouped by theme. It is not the per-commit file union that earlier dispatches used, and it says how often a theme appears in subjects, not how large the work was.

The themes that recur most: Rust and specification work (trust-task hardening patches, a Rust hold branch marked as uncompiled and not for merge, and build-venue repairs); the store and packages (the 1797 package-extraction rewrite and the follow-up fixes that made the rewritten repository resolve, including regenerated registries and a fix to a command-line load break); grants and authorisation (design records and red-first tests about how write and read authority cascades through knowledge domains, one landing that fixed a write-authority widening, and records of an owner ruling on checks that replace member-row domain lists); hive channels (design records for key rotation and invitations); calls and egress appear in the design-record subjects but far less often than the first four.

Most of the subjects in the week's landings are design-phase records and evidence notes on long-running branches, many of them correcting earlier notes by the same author. That is a description of what the subjects say, not a verdict on them.

An honest note on this week's pace

The counts above are the largest in this run of weeks, and much of the jump is not a week of new product work. A great deal of it is older branch work reaching the shared line, plus a large documentation and design-record pass and test relocation and repair (for example, test files renamed to describe behaviour rather than carry a roadmap number, and test files relocated after the packages left the repository). Commits are a measure of landing activity, not of value delivered, and one very large integration day (09-23, 1,607) shows how lumpy the shared line can be.

How the figures are counted. Commits are counted by committer date, UTC, over the post-rewrite history (the 1797 package-extraction rewrite rewrote git history), so they do not chain to the pre-September published dispatches; a different counting method would read differently.

What would prove this week wrong

  • Weather delivered between two real devices has not been seen. Until it is, the memory-only design is a design and a code path, not an observed behaviour.
  • The cluster paragraph is a read of landing subjects. A landing whose subject is vague, or a theme the subjects name differently, would shift the frequency; it is not a count of files or of effort.
  • The "much of it is older branch work" reading is an interpretation of the landing subjects. A history query that separates commits made in the window from commits that merely became reachable in it could change how much of the total is called new.
  • These figures do not chain to earlier dispatches. Read each week's numbers on their own.
