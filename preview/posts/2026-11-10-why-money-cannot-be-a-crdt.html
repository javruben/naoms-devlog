Why Money Cannot Be a CRDT

Order-free merge is perfect for a shared note and exactly wrong for a balance that must never go below zero

Technology Architect free November 10, 2026·3 min read
TL;DR CRDTs let replicas converge with no coordinator, which is why we like them for text. A balance that must never overdraw breaks their central assumption. Here is the argument, and the design it pushes us toward: fold value from an ordered, signed record instead of merging it.

We lean on CRDTs where they fit. Two people typing into one note should merge without a referee, and they do. So the obvious question about value is: why not store balances the same way? Every replica converges, nobody needs a central ledger, and the design stays local-first.

It does not work, and the reason is not an engineering inconvenience. It is a property of the math.

What a CRDT actually promises

A CRDT converges because its operations do not care about order. Apply the same set of updates in any sequence, any grouping, with duplicates, and every replica lands on the same state. The merge function itself is the ordering. That is strong eventual consistency: same updates received, same state, no coordinator.

The catch is built into the promise. It only holds if an operation's legality never depends on what came before it.

The counterexample

Alice has 10 units. Offline, she sends 10 to Bob. On another device, she sends 10 to Clara. Each transfer, looked at alone, is legal.

Now merge. Whichever transfer lands second becomes illegal, because the first already spent the money. Whether operation two is allowed depends on whether operation one applied. The operations do not commute, so the foundation of a CRDT is gone.

The literature is blunt about this. A counter that merely counts up and down is a fine CRDT: replicas agree on a number. But a counter that must never go negative cannot be enforced by order-free merge, because two concurrent withdrawals can each be legal and still jointly break the rule. Replicas agreeing on a number says nothing about whether the number respects the invariant. Convergence is not correctness.

So what do we do instead

You have two honest choices. Coordinate at spend time (a global ledger or consensus), or do not merge value at all and instead compute it from an ordered record. NAOMS has no sky-ledger to ask, so the design takes the second path.

Value events are signed facts written to the chain. A balance is never stored as a mergeable number. It is folded: a deterministic function reads the signed events in a deterministic order and produces the balance. Same events in, same balance out, on every replica. We borrow the good half of the CRDT recipe, a pure deterministic function of the input set, and drop the half that cannot enforce legality, the claim that order does not matter.

When concurrent entries do conflict, the design's answer is to allow them to be recorded, detect the violation deterministically afterwards, choose a deterministic loser by a rule nobody can grind, and account for the loss in the open rather than silently clamping it. That last part is the honesty axiom at work: value claims are signed and chained, and when an enforced amount differs from the requested one, both are recorded.

A separate layer of checks asks the question convergence cannot: does the total minted equal the sum of balances, and does anything sit below zero? Replicas agreeing is necessary. It is not sufficient, so correctness gets its own test.

Where CRDTs still belong

None of this is hostility toward CRDTs. They are the right tool for anything where "all replicas agree on a value" is the whole requirement and no order-dependent rule applies:

  • membership and grant sets, where "X is a member" is an add-only fact that commutes;
  • read and dismissal state, such as whether you have seen a notice;
  • labels, pins, and draft annotations on a card;
  • advisory fields derived from the fold, which are notices rather than authority.

Even there we keep one line firm: removal of a grant is a signed fact with a reason and an evidence link, not a silent tombstone, because the reason matters.

The design principle

Merge where merging is truthful. Order where order is the point. A shared note can lose the question of who typed first; a balance cannot. The chain gives value the ordered, authenticated history it needs, and the fold turns that history into a number every peer can reproduce for themselves, with no server telling them what they own.
