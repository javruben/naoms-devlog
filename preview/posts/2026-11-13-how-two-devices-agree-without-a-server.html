How Two Devices That Never Meet a Server Agree

Peer-to-peer sync in NAOMS: signed events, gossip, and convergence that never stops running

Technology Architect free November 13, 2026·4 min read
TL;DR NAOMS devices converge on shared state without any coordinating server. This post walks through what actually travels between daemons, how each message is checked on arrival, and why we deliberately have no second reconciliation machine.

Most apps keep two copies of your data in sync by putting a server in the middle. The server holds the official version, and every device defers to it. NAOMS has no such server. So the obvious question is: when two devices change things independently, how do they end up agreeing?

Clients only ever talk to home

The first design decision is about who talks to whom. A NAOMS client — the app on your screen — speaks only to the daemon running on your own machine. It never opens a connection to anyone else's computer. Daemons talk to each other, over an encrypted peer-to-peer transport built on QUIC, with relays available to help two machines behind firewalls find each other. On a local network, daemons can also discover one another automatically.

Discovery is deliberately cheap and ambient. Seeing that a peer exists grants nothing. What the trust graph decides is what actually replicates: relationships and memberships determine which histories flow to which peer, and which files are allowed to stream.

What travels is the history, not the state

Devices don't ship each other "the current value." They exchange chain events: signed, hash-linked records of what happened. Each event carries its author's signature and a link to the event before it. A receiving daemon can check an event on its own, with no help from whoever relayed it.

New events are announced over gossip, an epidemic style of broadcast where peers pass news along to peers. Gossip is for announcements and small events. Large files travel by a separate, direct, chunked path, gated by the same trust relationships.

Five checks before anything is accepted

Every gossip message goes through a series of checks, in order:

  1. The envelope. It must carry a valid signature and a fresh timestamp — a short window into the past and a small tolerance into the future.
  2. The sender. The sender must be you, one of your own devices, an active friend, or a co-member of the hive. A revoked identity is refused. If the daemon cannot read its revocation records, it treats the sender as revoked rather than guessing.
  3. The binding. The identity that signed the event must match the identity that signed the envelope, so a relay cannot pass someone else's message off as its own.
  4. The topic. Messages on a peer topic are checked against the peer; messages on a hive topic are checked against active membership in that specific hive.
  5. The rules of the chain. The event must satisfy the business rules for its kind of chain.

Outbound traffic is filtered the same way. A friendship chain goes only to that friend; hive and channel chains go only to co-members. If the daemon can't work out who is authorized, it sends to zero peers, not all of them.

  A["Device A\nsigns event"] -- gossip --> V{"Receiver checks:\nenvelope, sender,\nbinding, topic, rules"}
  V -- accepted --> B["Device B\nreplays event"]
  V -- refused --> X(("dropped"))
  B -. "catch-up replay for\nanything missed" .-> A

Convergence is a background process

Once accepted, events are replayed into local state the same way locally authored events are. Two devices that hold the same events and replay them arrive at the same state; that is the whole mechanism. Nobody needs a global ordering, because each history is written by an authority that orders its own events.

Peers that were offline, or that join late, catch up through targeted replay of what they missed. Hive membership events carry a note of who authorized them, so a new member's admission can still be accepted by a peer that hasn't yet seen the authorizing event.

Notice what's missing. There is no checkpoint scheme, no Merkle-tree comparison pass, no second machine that periodically reconciles everyone. Replication is events over gossip plus catch-up replay. If two writers genuinely claim the same position on a single-writer history, that is detected as a fork and repaired — it is not silently merged away. We kept the system small on purpose: a second reconciler is a second place for the truth to be decided.

Why this shape

It follows from the three axioms we build on. Wholeness: it all works on a LAN with no internet, and your own history stays appendable and queryable even when no peer is reachable — replication is background work, not a precondition. Honesty: every replicated byte is verifiable, because events carry signatures and hash links and gossip wraps them in signed envelopes. Mystery: no peer sees the whole. Sync is incremental, eventual, and tolerant of gaps. Divergence is the normal resting state; convergence is something that is always quietly happening.
