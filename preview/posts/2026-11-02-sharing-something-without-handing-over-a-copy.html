Sharing Something Without Handing Over a Copy

How a share becomes a scoped, per-relationship event with terms attached — and how it can end

Technology Architect free November 2, 2026·3 min read
TL;DR Most systems treat sharing as copying: once it leaves, it is gone. NAOMS treats a share as a named, per-person event with a level, terms and an end — and lets it stop, expire or be made unreadable.

In most software, "share" quietly means "copy and let go." You attach the file, you send the location, and from then on the other side holds something you can't scope, age or take back. NAOMS starts from a different question: what if a share were a relationship-shaped fact rather than a one-way copy?

Sharing is per person, never "everyone"

Every share is keyed to one connection or one group context. There is no "share with all my friends" switch underneath. A preference says: for this person, this category of information, at this level. Levels are chosen per domain (location, friends, groups), each with its own default terms. When settings conflict, the most specific one wins: a per-person override beats a group override, which beats your default profile, which beats the system default.

That specificity is the first half of "no hand-over." Nothing flows ambiently.

A share is an event, and it only fires on real change

A small engine watches your data. When something relevant changes, it waits a moment (so a burst of edits becomes one pass), then rebuilds what each affected person should see, using a plugin for that domain. Each plugin owns its own levels, default terms, and how to build and revoke the payload.

The engine then fingerprints the result and compares it to the fingerprint of the last thing it sent. Only if they differ does it append a new share event to the chain you and that person share. The first time, it just records the baseline. So the chain holds a clean story: what was shared, at which level, signed by whom, with what terms, and when.

  F --> G[That person only]

The commit on the chain, not a screen or a cache, is the authoritative record that a share happened.

Terms travel with the share

Each share carries terms, including an optional expiry and what should happen when it lapses. A periodic sweep looks for shares whose time has passed and, if the terms say delete, ends them. Forgetting is designed in: received location data, for example, expires rather than accumulating forever.

Three honest ways for a share to end

Tombstone. Content is replaced by a marker, but the chain's hashes stay intact, so history still verifies even though the content is gone. Ending a share also revokes any consent rules that depended on it.

Erasure on request. A data subject, or the node's owner, can ask for erasure. Every share originating from that subject is tombstoned, and a record of what was removed is written to the governance side of the chain.

Crypto-shred. For the strongest case, the key that unlocks a domain's encrypted shares can be destroyed. Without it, nothing can be re-encrypted for anyone new. This is the owner's act alone, it is irreversible once it runs, and it waits through a 72-hour cooling-off period during which it can be cancelled. A receipt is recorded when it completes.

That cooling-off is the design in miniature: the one action that cannot be undone is also the one with a built-in pause.

And publishing is the other kind of sharing

Not everything goes to a named person. When you want something public, the pages feature takes a folder of static files and publishes it to a branch of a chain you write to. Your own daemon serves it over plain HTTP, content-addressed so every byte can be checked against its hash. There is no outside host holding your site; the content lives on your chain.

Why it's built this way

Sharing as copying makes consent a one-time event. Sharing as a scoped, signed, expiring event makes it an ongoing relationship: a level you can change, terms that age, and endings you control. The price is some bookkeeping, which buys the ability to say what was shared, with whom, and what happened to it.
