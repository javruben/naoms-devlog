Trust Is a Reading Taken From Signed Edges

Why NAOMS keeps trust in relationships, computes it on demand, and never stores a score

Technology Architect free November 14, 2026·2 min read
TL;DR Trust in NAOMS belongs to relationships, not to people, and it is never stored as a number. This post explains the two kinds of trust, how it spreads through a graph, and where the system is honest about gaps.

Many systems hang a trust score on each user: a rating, a karma number, a verified badge. NAOMS refuses that, and the reasons come straight from how it models trust.

Trust belongs to a relationship

The design starts from one claim: trust is a property of relationships, not an attribute of beings. It runs along two axes:

  • Bilateral: two beings sign an agreement together (exchanging relationship cards, vouching, endorsements).
  • Collective: a group issues a scoped credential, such as a hive vouching for a role or a panel verifying a claim.

Both end up as edges in the same graph, backed by the same signed, append-only chain.

The bilateral rule matters: a mutual trust edge needs both parties' signatures. Saying "I trust you" alone does not manufacture a mutual relationship. Trust you declare is only a statement until the other side joins it.

Two kinds of trust, kept apart

The design separates trusted attributes ("I am X", which are self-declared and can be verified) from trust signals ("we recognise you as X", granted by others). They answer different questions, so they are not blended. Signals also make the signer name why they trust, which pushes honesty into the act of vouching.

A reading, not a rating

No single trust number is stored. Scores are computed on demand from the signed edges. The guiding line is: "the temperature is not the patient. It is a reading, not a rating." A stored score invites gaming and freezes someone into a label. A computed reading can only be as good as the edges behind it, and each edge carries its own proof, so it can be checked offline.

How trust travels

flowchart LR
  you((You)) -- signed --> a((A))
  a -- signed --> b((B))
  b -- signed --> c((C))
  you -. "strong" .-> a
  you -. "weaker" .-> b
  you -. "weaker still" .-> c

When the system needs an attestation score, it propagates trust outward through the graph, with influence fading by distance. This follows well-known social-graph approaches to trust; that general technique is background framing here rather than a NAOMS invention. A friend's word counts for more than a friend-of-a-friend's.

Notably, edges do not lose weight merely because time passes. An edge drops out of consideration when it is revoked or its stated validity ends. A "decay over time" idea was considered and is not implemented for trust edges.

Revocation reaches downstream

Trust is often derived: one credential unlocks another. Revoking a trust anchor invalidates everything derived from it at verification time, not just when it was issued. This closes the window where a stale anchor is still trusted. Revoking a trust credential likewise cascades through the capabilities derived from it.

Trust doesn't carry between contexts

Trust earned in one context does not automatically apply in another. The old approach of a single trust-level number was largely retired. Authority now comes from three things together: the state of the relationship handshake, the per-domain sharing level the person sets, and capability credentials. That keeps trust from becoming a master key.
