Outgoing, Not Incoming: Where the PII Gate Belongs

To stop private data escaping, guard the exit — not the entrance — and define the threat precisely or the gate becomes theater

Technology Architect free May 28, 2026·5 min read·test-discipline
TL;DR To keep personal data out of the datasets we share, the obvious move is to scrub on the way in — and it guards a door the leak doesn't have to use. The better gate stands where data actually leaves, in its final form, with a precise definition of what "personal" means. Two lessons that generalize well past test data.

If you want to stop something private from escaping, the question is not only what you check for. It's where you stand to check. Put the guard at the wrong door and it will wave the leak right through while diligently inspecting traffic that was never going anywhere.

NAOMS generates synthetic test datasets — "packs" — so that the system can be exercised against realistic shapes of data without needing real users' lives to do it. The packs get shared. Which means there's a real obligation: nothing personal should end up baked into a pack that travels. The question of late May was where to enforce that, and the answer that landed was a clean inversion of the obvious one.

The obvious place is the wrong place

The intuitive design is an incoming gate. As data arrives into the dataset-generation machinery, scan it for personally-identifying information and refuse anything that looks sensitive. Guard the entrance. It feels right because it's early — catch the problem before it's anywhere near a shareable artifact.

But trace what an incoming gate actually protects. It inspects data at ingestion and then trusts everything downstream. Every transform, every synthesis step, every join between the entrance and the moment a pack is written can introduce, recombine, or surface personal data that the entrance never saw in that form. The thing you ship is the snapshot you build at the end, not the bytes you let in at the start. An incoming gate guards a door the leak doesn't have to use.

There's a subtler failure too: an incoming gate is a gate on your inputs, which you arguably already control. The risk you actually care about is on your outputs — the artifact that leaves your trust boundary and lands in someone else's hands. Checking inputs to protect outputs is checking the wrong population.

The gate belongs where data leaves

The reform moved the PII gate to the outgoing side: the check now lives at the point where a snapshot is built from daemon state for export — the moment data is about to become a shareable pack — rather than at ingestion. Same intent, opposite door. You scan the thing that's actually leaving, at the last moment before it leaves, when it is in its final, shippable shape.

This is the same principle that makes output encoding the right defense against injection, rather than input filtering: you sanitize at the boundary that matters, in the context where the data will actually be used, because that's the only place you can see what's truly about to escape. Every intermediate transform is now downstream of trust and upstream of the gate — exactly the right order. Whatever those transforms did, the outgoing scan sees the result.

Paired with the gate move, there's a CLI to make the check a thing a human can run on demand — naoms dataset pii-scan — so "is this pack clean?" becomes a question you can ask directly about an artifact, not a property you have to take on faith from an ingestion step that ran long ago.

We're describing the architecture of the reform — the gate relocated to the outgoing snapshot-build boundary, plus the scan command — from the dated directive, not from a line-by-line read of the final landed change. This is a large body of work that was still building packs through this week; the PII gate move is one decision inside it, not a claim the whole effort was finished.

What counts as "personal" — and what doesn't

Moving the gate was only half the reform. The other half was tightening the definition, in both directions.

The sharper one: DIDs were removed from the PII pattern set. A DID — a decentralized identifier — is a public W3C identifier. It is designed to be shared; it's how identities are referenced across the network. Treating a DID as personally-identifying information to be scrubbed is a category error. It's public by construction. Flagging it doesn't protect anyone — it just trains the gate to cry wolf, and a gate that cries wolf is a gate people start ignoring. Removing the DID pattern made the scan more trustworthy by making it less trigger-happy, because every alert it does raise now means something.

In the other direction, the phone-number pattern was tightened — narrowed so it matches actual phone numbers rather than firing on every digit sequence that vaguely resembles one. Same goal as dropping DIDs: a gate is only useful if its hits are real. False positives and false negatives are both failures of a classifier, and a privacy gate that's wrong in either direction erodes the very trust it exists to provide. Loosen it and real data slips; tighten it carelessly and it becomes noise people route around.

The combined move — drop the thing that's public by design, sharpen the thing that's genuinely sensitive — is a single coherent stance: precision is a privacy feature. A gate that flags public identifiers and real secrets with equal alarm has no signal. One that knows the difference can actually be trusted to mean it.

The general lesson

Two principles fall out of this, and they generalize well past test datasets.

Guard the exit, not the entrance. When the thing you're protecting is what leaves your trust boundary, put the gate at the boundary where it leaves, in the data's final form. Input-side checks protect inputs; output-side checks protect outputs; and it's almost always the outputs you owe a duty of care to.

Define the threat precisely or the gate becomes theater. A privacy filter is a classifier, and a classifier with no precision is worse than none — it launders false confidence. DIDs are public; phone numbers are not; encoding that distinction is the design. The gate that knows what it's actually looking for is the only one anyone will keep trusting.

Both lessons are the same lesson, really, told twice: a guard is defined by where it stands and what it's looking for. Get either wrong and you have a checkpoint that inspects the innocent and waves the guilty through — busy, diligent, and pointed at the wrong door.
