Partner work starts with a shared document

How we work with other projects: a design document addressed to its author, sent back and forth until it is ours together, and why crediting people is part of the design rather than a footnote

Process Fellow Travelers free September 30, 2026·9 min read·meta
TL;DR Every time we work with another project we write one document, after the design and before the building, addressed to the person whose work it grows from. It says what we read, what we propose, what could go wrong and what we are asking, and it travels back and forth until it is shared. Here is how that goes, shown with three real examples, and how we try to make sure we honour the people behind an idea instead of mining it.

More and more of what we are building starts with somebody else's work. A community calendar that an organiser built to show regenerative gatherings on a map. A budgeting tool written so a community of experts can budget a whole region together. A standards group describing, in public, how to ask another party to do something consequential. Each of them is years of someone's thinking, and each of them arrived at our door as a person, not a dependency.

We have now done this enough times that a pattern has settled, and the pattern is worth writing down. It is simple to say: after the design, and before the building, we write one document, address it to the author, and send it. What happens to that document afterwards is the collaboration.

What the document is

It is a PDF. That is deliberate. A PDF is something a person can read on a train, print, annotate, forward to a colleague, and keep. It asks for no account and no install, and it does not change after it leaves our hands unless we send a new edition.

The documents are all built the same way, and the sameness is the point, so a reader who has seen one can find their way through the next.

It opens with who it is from, who it is to, the date, and the status. Not a cover page of branding: a small block saying plainly "FROM this person, TO that person, dated today, and here is what state the work is in". When a document is revised it says which edition it is and what changed since the last one.

It says what we read. Before we write to someone we read their work, and the document lists what we read and what we counted, so they can tell whether we understood it. Where we could not check something, it says so, in a label, at the point it appears.

It says what is decided and what is not. Every open choice is marked as a decision, and where there is a default that applies until the author answers, we state it. Nothing is slipped in as settled.

It says what could go wrong. We try to break our own design first and put the result in the document, including the parts we have not solved.

It asks, and it says the asks are not conditions. A document that feels like an ultimatum does not start a conversation. Ours say outright that a "no" is a useful answer.

Then it goes out, and the real work starts: their reply becomes the next edition.

Three documents, in their own words

These are the three documents themselves, with the pages that are not ready to share publicly removed (where that happens, a note after the cover says which pages). Scroll through each one here, or press Full screen to read it properly.

Trust Tasks: a letter to a standards group

Trust Tasks is a draft specification for describing consequential work between two parties, written by a task force. We wrote them a letter: 22 pages, dated 22 September, titled A first step toward a long friendship. It proposed two changes to their specification and put six questions to them, and its cover states that it makes no claim that our software conforms to anything.

The page to look at is near the end, "Six questions, and an open invitation", where the letter stops arguing and invites.

The editor answered the same day. Adopting their discipline, rather than claiming compatibility, has already turned up mistakes of ours, and those are now fixed. The letter did not ask for that. It made it possible.

The regen calendar: a document for someone who built the thing first

Andrew built a map of regenerative events. We want the people of each community to curate a shared calendar together, and his map is where the idea and the feel of it come from. So the design notes we sent are addressed to him, and they open with:

You built the events map this grows from.

The second edition runs to 15 pages (the public copy above leaves one out). It explains the goal in a sentence, how the calendar works for people, what the screens look like, what could go wrong, and what we need from him.

The screens are shown dark and light, on a desktop and on a phone, and the document is clear that they are mock-ups with invented people and events, and that the photographs are freely licensed and credited on the last page.

Its last section is the one that matters most for this article. It is called "Decisions for you", and it is, in its own words, "the only section that asks anything of you". There are six, each with options and a plain statement of what follows from each.

Two of the decisions are about what we would be taking. Where the question of reusing code was not settled, the document says what we will do until he answers: "no code is taken from the repository." Ideas and data are not held up by it. And about the events he had gathered, it says the data holds the names and contact handles of the people who submitted them, and that "we will not carry either across unexamined." That is the point where an integration can quietly turn into extraction: the thing you are offered has other people's names inside it. The document stops there and asks.

It closes: "We would far rather shape it with you than around you."

WikiBudget: a whole design, offered before any code

Brad's WikiBudget lets a community of experts write a trusted, living budget for a whole area together. Our document to him is 42 pages, and it is unusually honest about where things stand, on its cover: nothing is implemented. What exists is a design, a mock-up of 56 screens, and 239 tests written before the code, every one of which fails today by design, because the feature they describe does not exist yet.

Early in the document is the one idea that most needs his review (on the page called "How cross-hive works"): how two groups, each with their own members and rules, can ask each other for money without either one speaking for the other.

Then it shows the app itself, screen by screen, so the author can tell whether we have understood what his tool is for.

Why the back and forth matters

A document sent once is a pitch. A document that returns with changes, in the author's hand, is a design. We number the editions, say what changed, and keep the earlier ones.

Because it is addressed to a person, it also has a person on the other end who can tell us we have it wrong. More than once the document says that a point is our own reading and asks to be told if it is wrong, and it labels things we believe but have not confirmed. We would rather be corrected in an early edition than be confidently mistaken in the finished software.

Extraction is the failure we are guarding against

Extraction is easy to fall into and hard to see from the inside. You read someone's public work, it is good, you build on it, and you never tell them. No one did anything malicious. But something was taken: the idea, the years behind it, and the people whose names were in the data.

Our values point the opposite way, so the practice has to as well. In each of the documents above, these are the habits that do the work:

  • Address the person. The author is the recipient, not a source in a footnote.
  • Say what we read, and credit it. Including the photographers.
  • Ask before taking. Code, data, and anything with people's details in it. Until the author answers, we take ideas and nothing else.
  • Ask, and never make the asking a condition of the collaboration.
  • Offer the design before the building, so the author can change it while changing it is cheap.

There is a harder half. The person who answers our letter is almost never the only person whose work is in the thing. A standards document has a whole task force behind it. An events map has years of submissions from the people who organised the events. A budget tool has the experts who have used and shaped it. Each of those people has a past, and most of them will never read our document.

We cannot name all of them today, and we will not pretend we can.

Where the infrastructure comes in

This is where what we are building is meant to help, and we want to be careful to say what exists and what does not.

What exists: the research notes on every outside project we have learned from are kept alongside the code, more than a hundred of them, and many of our design items carry a file listing what they draw on. Records in NAOMS are signed and linked so that an entry cannot be quietly changed after the fact, which means a contribution, once recorded, stays recorded.

What does not exist yet: the part that turns that history into recognition. Contribution accounting is still design work, not software. So we can say what we are aiming at, but not claim we have arrived.

The aim is that, when the infrastructure is finished, the historical artifacts of a project, the ideas, the designs, the edits, and the people who made them, can carry full attribution. Over time, those contributions can be recognised, and the recognition can carry funding with it, the kind sometimes called flow funding, which follows what has been contributed instead of arriving as a one-off. Everyone who brought a project, and everyone who helped make it what it was when it reached us, would then be in the record, not just whoever happened to reply to our letter.

We are keeping that in mind now, at the point where the shared document is written, because it is the cheapest time to get the habit right. A record of who did what can only be as good as the care taken when the work first arrives.

What is in the pipeline

Each of these is at a different stage, and none of them is finished software.

  • Wiki budgets. The 42-page document above; design and tests written, code not yet.
  • The calendar map. The 15-page notes above; awaiting the author's answers to six decisions.
  • Trust Tasks. Our letter is sent and answered, and we are adopting the discipline one checked claim at a time.
  • EVR. A way for a community to build and run its guilds on NAOMS, with guilds as groups, reputation as verifiable credentials and shared value tracked alongside.
  • Consensual. A friend's project; we have written an analysis of how the two fit and a proposal.
  • Reticulum. A network that needs very little; we have an analysis and a first-phase design for bringing it in next to the networking we already use.
  • Pathways. Reusable, versioned procedures; we are designing a marketplace for them that nobody owns.
  • And the ones that came before, such as the Proof of Social Learning work and the first steps with Aqua, and the projects we have honoured in earlier Fellow Travelers posts.

If you are working on something and think we should be talking, this is what writing to us looks like from the other side: you will get a document, it will have your name at the top, and it will ask you what we have got wrong.
