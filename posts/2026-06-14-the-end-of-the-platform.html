The Morning the Company in the Middle Wasn't There

A small scene from the world we're building toward — where the photos, the chat, and the group's rules all run on the people's own machines, and there's no middle left to ask permission from

Vision Philosopher free June 14, 2026·2 min read·vision
TL;DR Instead of arguing that the extractive platform was only ever one shape the internet could take, here is a small ordinary morning in the world where the middle is simply gone — and the only thing you notice is what you got back.

It's a Tuesday a few years on. Devon spills coffee on the kitchen counter and, before wiping it up, opens the photos from his daughter's birthday to send three of them to his mother.

He doesn't think about where the photos are. They're just here — on his phone, on the laptop in the other room, the same pictures in both places because his own devices passed them to each other directly overnight. He picks three, taps his mother's name, and they go straight to her. Not up to a server and back down. Across, between two homes, over a link only the two of them can read. No company saw the birthday. No company holds the copy. If he loses the phone tomorrow, the photos are still on the laptop, still his, and his mother already has hers.

At lunch the climbing club he runs needs a decision: new bouldering wall or fix the old one. He posts the question to the club's space. The space is the club's own thing — its member list, its chat, its little history of every vote it's ever taken — living on the members' machines, not rented from anyone. People weigh in through the afternoon. By evening there's a tally, signed by everyone who voted, that nobody could quietly edit after the fact. No admin holds a master switch. No terms of service sits above them. The club governs the club.

That night Devon finishes a freelance edit for someone across the country. She marks down that he did the work and that it was good — a plain record, held between the two of them, that value changed hands. It isn't money and it isn't a platform's points. It's an honest account of a thing he did and what it was worth, and it stays theirs.

None of this feels like a stand against anything. It feels like a normal Tuesday.

Here's what the scene quietly reveals. There was always a company in the middle — holding the photos, owning the club's chat, sitting between Devon and the person who paid him, taking its cut of all of it. In this Tuesday it simply isn't there. Nobody fought it off. There was just nothing left for it to hold, so it was never in the room. And nothing was lost when it left: the photos sent, the club decided, the work counted. What went missing was only the toll.

That's the end of the platform. Not the end of connection — the end of paying a stranger for the right to have it.

An honest word: not all of Devon's Tuesday exists yet. The pieces where your identity, your records, your direct connections, and your group's self-rule live on your own machines are the direction we're walking, one verifiable step at a time. The part where value circulates among members at scale is mostly design and intention today, not a finished economy. We'd rather you trust the stones we've laid than believe in a building that isn't up.
