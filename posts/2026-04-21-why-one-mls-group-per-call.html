One Locked Room for the Whole Call, Not One Per Pair

Why a group call uses a single shared encryption group (MLS) with one owner — and why the obvious alternative is quietly broken

Technology Architect paid · premium preview April 21, 2026·7 min read·messaging
TL;DR When several people join one encrypted call, letting everyone set up the encryption gives you a pile of conflicting groups for a single call. The fix is one rule: one shared locked room per call, with exactly one host who opens it and lets everyone in. Here's why that has to be the shape, grounded in the fan-out piece that shipped 2026-04-21 and the single-owner fix that followed.

Here's a question that sounds trivial until you try to build it: when three people are on an end-to-end encrypted call, how many encryption groups are there?

The obvious-but-wrong answer is "one per pair." Alice↔Bob, Bob↔Carol, Alice↔Carol — three pairwise encrypted links, like three separate phone lines. It feels safe. It is, in fact, a quietly broken design, and on 2026-04-21 we shipped the piece of NAOMS calling that proves why and does it the right way: one MLS group per call, with a single owner.

This is a concept piece about a deceptively small decision. The implementation detail — that one being creates the group and fans out the invitations to everyone else — is three sentences. The reason it has to be exactly that, and not the seemingly-fine alternatives, is the whole point.

The naive design and why it rots

Start with per-pair encryption, because it's what most people reach for first.

If every pair of participants negotiates its own encryption, a three-person call is three independent secure channels. Audio from Alice to Bob is encrypted under the Alice↔Bob key; Alice to Carol under a different key. No single shared secret. Each link is end-to-end encrypted. What's wrong with that?

Three things, and they get worse as the call grows:

It's N-squared. Three people is three links. Five people is ten. Ten people is forty-five. Every participant has to establish and maintain a separate encryption session with every other participant, and every audio frame you send has to be encrypted separately for each recipient. The key-management cost grows quadratically for a thing that should feel like one conversation.

There's no shared notion of "the call." With pairwise links, "who is in this call" is not a fact anyone holds. It's an emergent property of which pairs happen to have channels open. There's no single source of truth for membership, which means there's no clean answer to "did everyone leave?" or "is this person actually authorized to be here?" You've scattered the one fact that matters most.

Membership changes are a renegotiation storm. When a fourth person joins, per-pair means three new pairwise handshakes. When someone leaves, you have to tear down their links individually and hope nobody missed the memo. There's no atomic "the group changed" event — just a flurry of independent updates that can disagree with each other.

The deeper problem underneath all three is the one this whole project keeps running into: you've let multiple parties each own a piece of shared state, and now they can disagree. That's the same failure mode as two writers racing on a chain, or three nodes each thinking they're the membership authority. Pairwise encryption isn't wrong because the crypto is weak. It's wrong because it has no owner.

One group, one owner

The design NAOMS landed is a single MLS group for the entire call, named for the call itself — a per-call group — with one rule layered on top that makes it converge: only the call's creator creates the group, and only the creator fans out the invitations.

The piece that shipped on 2026-04-21 — MLS welcome fan-out at three participants — is precisely this, and the change describes the mechanism with unusual clarity. The fix is "honest-by-invariant": it verifies that the existing two-peer MLS handshake — the 2-party "dance" NAOMS already had from earlier work — composes naturally at N peers. In its own words: Alice runs one 2-party dance per joiner against a single per-call group; joiners each process their own welcome.

Read that twice, because it's the whole idea. There is one group. Alice (the creator) does one MLS handshake per person who joins — but always against the same group, not a fresh group each time. Each joiner receives an MLS "welcome" message and processes it to enter the group. The result is a single shared encryption state that everyone is a member of, built up one handshake at a time, with one being driving the whole thing.

The companion design from the same day names the remaining pieces this unlocks — per-channel chain-signer shares, the channel ceremony driver, per-peer QUIC stream bookkeeping — and notes they can each ship independently now that the group-membership invariant is settled. That's the signature of getting the core decision right: the hard part, once owned, decomposes the rest of the work instead of entangling it.

Proving it converges: the N=3 invariant

A design that "should compose" and a design that is proven to compose are different things, and the Honesty axiom doesn't let us ship the first one wearing the second one's clothes. So the 2026-04-21 piece didn't just assert N-party composition — it codified the convergence as a test.

The mechanism: a new per-participant bridge-state snapshot/restore harness lets the test stand up three independent MLS endpoints, run the creator's one-dance-per-joiner fan-out, and then assert that all three participants converge on the same group state. The verification is concrete — the three-participant welcome-fan-out test passing, the original two-peer handshake test still green as a regression guard, and a multi-party test confirming the N-group convergence. The point of the regression line is subtle and important: the N-party fan-out had to be the same handshake the two-peer path already used, not a parallel implementation. One mechanism, proven to scale, not two mechanisms that might drift.

This is also why switching the native MLS engine on by default earlier the same day is load-bearing under this piece. The convergence had to be proven on the real native MLS path — the one production uses — not against a simulated stand-in that might converge for reasons the real protocol wouldn't. A convergence proof against a fake is a proof about the fake.

The single-owner rule that closes the race

There's one more failure the single-group design has to defend against, and it's the one that gives this article its title. If there's one group per call, the next question is: who creates it?

If the answer is "whoever gets there first," you've reintroduced the race you just removed. Three participants all trying to be the founder of the per-call group means three candidate groups for one call — conflicting groups, exactly the thing we were avoiding, now hiding inside the "single group" design.

So the rule, landed in the mesh single-group milestone on 2026-04-26, is unambiguous: only the creator creates the MLS group, and only the creator fans out the call offer. Everyone else joins. There is one being who owns the group's existence and one being who owns the invitation fan-out, and it's the same being — the person who started the call. Membership has a single source of truth because group creation has a single owner. The result: mesh per-pair audio coming out symmetric and the integration suite fully green — the observable payoff of removing the last place two parties could disagree about whose group is real.

(That mesh fix shipped on the 26th, not the 21st — we're describing it here because it's the natural completion of the 21st's single-group concept. The convergence proof is the 21st's; the single-owner enforcement is the 26th's. Both are real, both are dated, and together they're the answer to the title.)

Why it's built this way

The whole arc — from "one group per call" to "one owner per group" — is the same principle the rest of NAOMS is built on, applied to encrypted audio: pick one honest owner for a piece of shared state instead of hoping concurrent writers agree.

Per-pair encryption hoped the pairs would add up to a coherent call. They don't — they add up to N-squared key management, no shared membership truth, and a renegotiation storm on every join. One MLS group per call replaces all of that with a single shared secret, a single membership fact, and atomic group changes. And the single-owner-creates rule replaces the last hidden race — who founds the group — with one designated being.

The crypto in both designs is strong. The difference is entirely about ownership of state. That's not a calling insight; it's the project's whole thesis, showing up in the place where getting it wrong would have leaked the most intimate data we carry. One call, one group, one owner. Everyone else is welcome — literally, via an MLS welcome message — to join.
