Note: We Added It, Reverted It, Then Added It Again

Why backing out a not-ready change fast is the honest move, not an embarrassment

Process Confession free April 5, 2026·2 min read·trust
TL;DR Backing a change out fast isn't failure — it's the cleanest thing you can do with something that isn't ready. Here's the night we added a feature, reverted it 36 seconds later, shipped it for good that evening, and kept the whole messy trail on purpose.

The git log for the evening of 2026-04-05 contains a small, honest comedy. Read in order, by timestamp:

add proof-on-platform verification UI for social accounts — 21:03:56

Revert "add proof-on-platform verification UI for social accounts" — 21:04:32

add proof-on-platform verification UI for social accounts — 23:58:38

Added at 21:03. Reverted thirty-six seconds later at 21:04. Re-added, same title, nearly three hours later at 23:58. The feature is the proof-on-platform UI for greencheck — the part of NAOMS where you prove you control a social account (a GitHub handle, a Mastodon profile) and the system posts or checks a proof on that platform to verify it.

We are keeping this note short because the lesson is short.

A revert is not an apology. The instinct, when you see a revert thirty-six seconds after a feature commit, is to read shame into it — they shipped something broken and panicked. That is not what a revert is. A revert is a single, atomic, fully-reversible statement: "not yet, and here is exactly the state we're going back to, with no ambiguity about what changed." It is the cleanest thing you can do when something isn't ready. The messy alternative — quietly editing the half-built thing in place, leaving the tree in a smeared in-between state nobody can describe — is what actually causes pain. The revert kept the tree honest.

🟠 GUESS, labeled as such: the materials for this week do not retain a chat log or a commit body explaining why the 21:03 commit was pulled back at 21:04 (the records for this window are gone — see below). The thirty-six-second gap strongly suggests it was caught immediately — a broken build, a wrong base, a merge collision — rather than a considered design reversal. But we do not know the reason, and we will not invent one. What we know is the shape: add, revert, re-add, all on the same night.

And then it landed. The honest, important half: the re-add at 23:58 stuck. The greencheck verification expansion reached its finished, celebrated state; the proof-on-platform UI shipped. The revert was mid-iteration, not a final defeat. This is the difference between a scar and a wound: the feature is in the product; the log just remembers that it took two tries.

Why leave the false start in history at all? Because squashing it away would be a small dishonesty — the same instinct this whole project is built to resist. The real story of building anything is add, revert, add again, and a git history that pretends otherwise is lying about how the work actually went. The Mystery axiom says forgetting is a feature; the Honesty axiom says you don't get to forget selectively to look better. We forgot the why (the records rotated away). We kept the what (three commits, in order, timestamps intact). That is the honest trade.

The lesson, one line:

Reverting fast is not failing. It's the most honest thing you can do with a commit that isn't ready — and the log keeping the scar is a feature, not an embarrassment.
