Seventeen surfaces, redesigned together

The feature: photos — everything from the grid you land on to the panel that tells you about a single picture.

Before: each surface had arrived at its own moment and carried its own conventions. Now: seventeen of them share one design, landed together on 7 June and faithful to the approved mock: shell, river, places, people, events in two layouts, stories, studio, sharing, editing and the inspector. (Photos, redesigned)

A redesign that stops at the front door is a reskin. The surfaces people judge a product by are rarely the ones they spend time in. Landing the shell alone would have made the first screen agree with the mock and every screen behind it disagree with the first — which reads worse than leaving all of them alone.

Doing seventeen at once is expensive and it is the cheaper of the two options over any horizon longer than a month. A partially converted interface has to carry both vocabularies, so every subsequent change has to be made twice and decided twice, and the conversion that was supposed to end never does.

What is still missing. The surfaces landed; the verification did not finish. This is labelled partial for that reason, and the honest description is that the redesign is in and being checked rather than done.

Saying yes and being ignored

The feature: NAOMS asks permission before it does anything sensitive, then writes down your yes, and checks for that note before it acts.

Before: the two halves had drifted. One filed the yes under one name, the other looked for it under a different one, so you could grant permission, watch the grant succeed, and find the feature silently shut. Now: the names are tied together, and the tests fail loudly when they come apart.

A contract between two halves of a system needs one name, enforced. Where each half spells the same thing its own way, the two agree exactly as long as one person holds both in their head — and drift the moment that stops being true. Nothing announces the drift, because each half is behaving correctly on its own terms.

This failure also fails in the safe direction, which is precisely why it survived. Permission not found means the action does not happen, so nothing breaks, nothing is logged as an error, and no data is harmed. The cost is entirely borne by a person who did what was asked and got nothing, and who has no way to tell a refused feature from a broken one.

What it cost to prove. Our own tests had been too polite to catch it: they granted permission and checked the grant, rather than granting permission and then checking that the guarded action ran. The fix tied the names together and made the test fail loudly, which is the part that keeps it fixed.

What this means, in plain terms

Five general lessons this week's work paid for, each learned by getting something wrong first.

Convert every surface, or carry two vocabularies forever

Seventeen photo surfaces landed one design together rather than one surface at a time. A half-converted interface forces every later change to be decided twice, once per convention, and the conversion never reaches an end. A migration done in slices has to be finished on a schedule, or the slices become the architecture.

One name, or the two halves drift

Permission was written under one name and read under another. Each half was correct in isolation, so nothing reported a fault, and a granted permission simply did nothing. Where two components must agree on an identifier, make the agreement checkable — the alternative is two components that were both right.

A test can be too polite to find the bug

The suite granted a permission and asserted the grant. It never asserted that the guarded action then ran, which is the only thing the person actually wanted. Assert the outcome the user came for, not the step you happened to implement last.

The first witness from outside is an instrument you cannot build

Jethro, working from his own copy of the code, returned NAOMS's first bug report from beyond our own circle within a day of picking up a real task — and it was a good one. A team testing its own product shares its own blind spots; the first outsider does not, and that is worth more than the fix they bring.

Say what you use, not what you are not

Sweeping out references to a prior external home, we found governance rules whose entire content was a prohibition. In the owner's words: we do not specify all the things we are not. we only specify what we do use. A rule that defines you by what you reject is a dependency on the thing you rejected — you have to keep naming it to keep refusing it.

From the workshop

Don't Define Yourself by What You're Not

What began as moving where the repository is mirrored turned into a small lesson about identity. Sweeping out stale references, we found governance rules whose entire content was a prohibition — "this is not that system," "do not use that style."

The direction that came back: we do not specify all the things we are not; we only specify what we do use. A rule that says "we are not X" feels like clarity, but it's really a dependency on X — you have to keep naming the thing to keep rejecting it, and every mention is a thread back to it.

So we deleted the negative rules rather than rewrite them. Say what you use, build what you believe in, and let your refusals be silent and implicit. There's more freedom — and less to maintain — in simply stating what you are.

From the workshop

The Day Jethro Found the Bug

For a long time we were the only ones using NAOMS for real. This week that changed: Jethro — a human collaborator based in Nigeria, working from his own copy of the code — took a real task and, within a day, handed back the first bug report NAOMS ever got from outside our own circle.

The bug was a good one: the permission drift described above, where a granted yes was filed under one name and looked for under another. Our own tests had been too polite to catch it. Jethro root-caused it, wrote a first fix and a clear report; we tied the names together for good and made the tests fail loudly next time.

The thing we got that day wasn't the fix — it was the first outside witness, proof there's a wider we here now. Thank you, Jethro. First of many, we hope.

What would prove this week's photos claim wrong

Four things this report has only half-proven, each stated as a commitment rather than as a task.

  • Seventeen surfaces landed; none of them is verified yet. Faithful to the approved mock is a statement about intent at landing time. The verification pass that would make it a statement about behaviour has not run to completion.

  • The files view has no capture of its own. A surface with no native screenshot has not been looked at the way a user looks at it, and this report is not able to show you one.

  • A hang is still buildable. Two paths that could block the main loop were moved out of it. The goal is a structural check that makes the shape impossible to write, and that check does not exist.

  • One outside witness is one. Jethro found a real bug in a day because he did not share our assumptions. Everything he did not touch is still tested by people who do.
