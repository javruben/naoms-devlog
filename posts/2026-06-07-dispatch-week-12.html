A ground-up photos redesign across seventeen surfaces — and a desktop-style Finder for your files.

Process Dispatch free June 7, 2026·7 min read·meta
TL;DR Here's what shipped for you this week: your photos experience was redesigned from the ground up across seventeen surfaces, files now live in nested folders you browse like a desktop Finder, your laptop can stay awake mid-build, and you can search every command's help text to find the verb you half-remember. Plus two honest infra fires worked through — a 443 GB disk emergency and a host at load 115.

A loud week. Two big themes carried it: an enormous mechanical refactor campaign that moved code between a hundred-plus files overnight to get oversized modules under a size cap, and two genuine infra fires — a repo-wide source-control cutover and a disk-space emergency with twenty minutes on the clock. In between, real product shipped: a ground-up photos redesign, a Finder-style files UI, two small CLI affordances, and steady progress on making a daemon hang structurally impossible. Here's the honest accounting.

Broke & fixed (honest)

  • 443 GB of coverage trash, twenty minutes to find it. The disk filled to ~400 MB free and the garbage collector wasn't reaping the cause. The owner's message captures the stakes exactly: "there is no time to wait for a task. we are at 400MB left." Root cause: end-to-end-test coverage directories piling up in a temp path the collector wasn't reaching — "that ~443 GB is the coverage trash." Found, cleared, and the collector's reach extended. Fixed.
  • The host hit load 115. Leftover headless Chrome processes from finished E2E tests, which the reaper wasn't clearing, drove a sixteen-core machine to seven times oversubscribed. Cleaned by hand (load fell to 5), and the reaper's gap identified. Fixed (reaper category landed-and-watching).
  • The merge discipline broke main. A hotfix removed duplicate symbols left by a particular kind of 3-way merge — the integration machinery itself introduced a breakage and had to clean up after itself. Plus two explicit rollbacks of broken changes and a peer-to-peer networking wedge root-caused and fixed.

Concept of the week — the "byte-move"

A byte-move is a refactor with zero semantic change: you relocate code from one file to another to get a module under a size cap, and a diff-scoped type-check proves that nothing else moved — no behavior changed, only addresses. It sounds trivial, and each one is. The interesting part is the scale: we ran roughly a hundred of them in a single night, mechanically, to bring oversized boot and transport modules under the project's file-size limit. It's the software equivalent of reorganizing a warehouse without changing the inventory — boring by design, and only safe because it's boring: the moment a "byte-move" changes behavior, it isn't one, and the type-check is there to catch exactly that.

From the workshop

Don't Define Yourself by What You're Not

What began as moving where the repository is mirrored turned into a small lesson about identity. Sweeping out stale references, we found governance rules whose entire content was a prohibition — "this is not that system," "do not use that style." The direction that came back: we do not specify all the things we are not; we only specify what we do use. A rule that says "we are not X" feels like clarity, but it's really a dependency on X — you have to keep naming the thing to keep rejecting it, and every mention is a thread back to it. So we deleted the negative rules rather than rewrite them. Say what you use, build what you believe in, and let your refusals be silent and implicit. There's more freedom — and less to maintain — in simply stating what you are.

From the workshop

The Day Jethro Found the Bug

For a long time we were the only ones using NAOMS for real. This week that changed: Jethro — a human collaborator based in Nigeria, working from his own copy of the code — took a real task and, within a day, handed back the first bug report NAOMS ever got from outside our own circle. The bug was a good one. NAOMS asks permission before it does anything sensitive, then writes down your yes; later it checks for that note before acting. The two halves had quietly drifted apart — one filed the yes under one name, the other looked for it under a different one — so you could grant permission, watch it succeed, and the feature stayed silently shut. Our own tests had been too polite to catch it. Jethro root-caused it, wrote a first fix and a clear report; we tied the names together for good and made the tests fail loudly next time. The thing we got that day wasn't the fix — it was the first outside witness, proof there's a wider we here now. Thank you, Jethro. First of many, we hope.

From the workshop

Names Over Numbers

A change that altered no behaviour and still made the system better: our internal workflows went from opaque numbers to descriptive names — the bug procedure is now called the bug procedure, the new-feature one says what it is in the reading of it. A number tells you nothing; you look it up every time, because an arbitrary number never means anything and the mind refuses to retain it. A name carries its meaning instead of pointing at it. That sounds cosmetic until you remember this project is operated overwhelmingly by readers — software agents acting on what they read — and at that scale every arbitrary number you replace with a self-describing name removes one small chance to misunderstand. Honest texture: it didn't arrive as a calm insight over coffee but as a sharp course-correction under load. Both are true.
