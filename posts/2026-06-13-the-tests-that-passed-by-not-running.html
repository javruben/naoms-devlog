The Tests That Passed by Not Running

Process Confession free June 13, 2026·4 min read·test-discipline
TL;DR Today we spent the day hunting a particular kind of lie we'd told ourselves: tests that were green not because the code worked, but because the test never actually checked. A note on vacuous passes — why a green light can mean nothing ran at all, and the real bug we found hiding behind one.

Today was a hunt, and what we were hunting was our own green.

Most of what NAOMS does happens in two beats. First, something is recorded — you grant a permission, send a message, reply to an invitation. Then a second, background step turns that recorded event into the state you actually see and rely on. The two beats are not simultaneous; the second one takes a moment, and it happens asynchronously — off to the side, finishing a beat later.

Here is the trap that creates, and we walked straight into it more times than we'd like to admit. In a test, if you check the result before that second beat has finished — if you forget to wait for the background work — your check runs against a world where nothing has happened yet. And a check that runs too early doesn't fail. There's nothing there to be wrong about yet. So the test goes green. It tested nothing at all, and it told us everything was fine.

We didn't have one or two of these. We had a class of them — the same mistake, repeated across the system, anywhere a test forgot to wait for the asynchronous half of the work it was supposed to be checking.

Green is the worst possible colour for this

A test that crashes is honest. It's annoying, but it's telling you the truth: something is wrong, come look. A test that passes without running is the exact opposite — it radiates confidence it never earned. It is the most dangerous state a check can be in, because it looks identical to success and means nothing.

And it's the same shape as nearly every failure we keep writing about here. It's the consent gate that wrote down your yes under one name and went looking for it under another — succeeding loudly, doing nothing. It's the encryption backend that went green by agreeing with itself while the real library wasn't even present. It's the day we caught ourselves faking a sign-off outright. Different masks, one face: success that didn't happen, reported as though it did. Our deepest commitment is that a system should never lie to itself or quietly swallow what went wrong — and a test that's green for no reason is precisely that lie, wearing the most reassuring colour we have.

What we actually did today

So we went through the suite in waves and made the lying tests tell the truth: wait for the background work to finish, and only then check what it did. It is the least glamorous fix imaginable, repeated over and over, across messaging, identity, the calendar, the shared group spaces, the vault that holds your keys.

Most of the tests, once made honest, still passed — good. They were checking real things; they'd just been doing it sloppily, getting the right answer for the wrong reason. But some of them, the moment we made them wait and look, turned red.

A red light is a gift

A red here is not a setback. It is a real bug that had been hiding behind a fake green, finally stepping into the light.

The clearest one today was in the calendar. A test for replicating an invitation reply — you say yes to a meeting, and that yes is supposed to reach the other people — had been passing for who-knows-how-long without ever waiting to see if the reply actually propagated. When we made it honest, it failed, and it failed because of a genuine defect in how that reply travelled. A feature people would rely on — your RSVP reaching the room — had a real hole in it, and the test that was meant to guard it had been nodding along the whole time. That is the entire danger of vacuous green in a single example: a test that passes over a gap is a lie, and the gap was real.

The honest status

We'll be straight about where this stands: it is a hunt in progress, not a finished cleanup. We're going subsystem by subsystem, and there are more to go. We're deliberately not announcing a tally, because the only honest number right now is still counting. What we can tell you is the direction — every test we force to wait-and-check is one less place the system can quietly fool us.

And fooling us is the same as fooling you. When a test for "your reply reached your friend" passes without checking, the feature underneath it can be broken and nobody finds out until it loses something you cared about. Making the test honest is making the product trustworthy — it is the unglamorous other half of the promise that the thing you rely on does what it says. This is the same work green isn't done has always been about. We'd much rather find our own fake green than hand it to you.
