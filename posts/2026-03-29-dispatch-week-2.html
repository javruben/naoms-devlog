Four things changed this week

One per major area. Together they are the trust, consent and encryption layer arriving at once — and the database underneath everything was rebuilt while it happened.

1

Your memories are encrypted on disk

Encryption is applied field by field, so only you can read what is stored, with a standing guarantee that you can always reach your own data.

2

Nothing is shared unless you say yes

Reads and shares are denied until a signed grant allows them, and every grant lands in an append-only trail that cannot be quietly edited afterwards.

3

You decide exactly who you trust, and for what

Relationships became explicit, typed connections you can reason about rather than an ambient membership list — and they are capped at a human number of close ties.

4

Group spaces reached the open internet

Hives moved off the local network. Gossip, lazy sync, backup and compaction carried them from a three-machine proof to the wider world.

Memories that only you can read

The feature: the memories NAOMS stores for you are encrypted where they sit on disk, so the file on your machine is not readable by anything that finds it.

Before: stored memories were readable to whatever could open the store. Now: they are encrypted with per-field AES-256-GCM, and the key situation is arranged so that you can always reach your own data.

Encryption at rest answers one specific question: what someone learns by getting hold of the file. It says nothing about what a running program with the keys can see, and nothing about what you choose to share. Those are the next two sections' problem, and the three only work as a set.

Encrypting per field rather than per file is a deliberate trade, and it costs something. A single encrypted blob is simpler and hides more. It also has to be decrypted wholesale before anything can be looked up inside it, which is fatal for a system whose entire job is searching what it remembers.

What was built. Each field is encrypted on its own, so the parts of a memory that need to be queryable can be handled separately from the parts that only ever need to be read back. The unit of protection matches the unit of use.

One property is easy to miss. Encryption here is a property of the store, not of a mode you switch on for sensitive items — there is no second, unprotected tier for ordinary memories.

The mode matters as much as the cipher. AES-256-GCM is authenticated encryption, which means a field that has been altered fails to decrypt rather than decrypting into something plausible and wrong. Silent corruption is the failure a memory system can least afford, because nothing downstream would know to doubt the result.

What this does not do is hide the shape of the store. Encrypting field by field protects the contents of each field; it does not conceal how many memories exist or how they are laid out. That is the price of keeping the store usable while encrypted, and it is a price rather than an oversight.

The guarantee that matters more than the cipher

An encryption scheme has two failure modes and only one of them gets attention. The famous one is somebody else reading your data. The quiet one is you being unable to.

Locked-out is the failure nobody demonstrates, because it looks like nothing happening.

The second is far more common and far more damaging in a personal system, because there is no support desk holding a spare copy. A memory you cannot decrypt is not protected; it is destroyed, with extra steps.

This week's work carries an explicit guarantee that your own access survives — that the owner of the data can always reach it. Naming the guarantee alongside the algorithm is the difference between a security feature and a data-loss bug in a security feature's clothing.

There is a second thing the guarantee buys, less obvious than recovery. It makes the encryption safe to turn on by default, because a scheme that can strand its owner has to be optional, and an optional protection protects roughly nobody.

Memory that only you can read covers the field-level scheme.

Four layers, each of which can be pulled

The feature: deciding who can see what, and having every one of those decisions be explicit, revocable, and recorded.

Before: access was ambient — being on a list was the permission. Now: access is denied by default and granted by four independent layers, any one of which can be removed to stop it.

Ambient permission is the normal way software does this, and it fails in a predictable direction. Names accumulate on lists, lists outlive the reason they were made, and nobody is ever asked to reconfirm. The failure is not that the wrong person was added; it is that the right person was never removed.

Default-deny is a sentence anyone can say and a property very few systems have. The test is not whether a request without permission is refused. The test is what happens to a request nobody thought about, and the only structure that answers it well is one where absence of a rule means no.

What was built. Four layers, each independently revocable: a verified identity, an explicit trust edge to that identity, a signed consent rule granting a specific scope, and a scoped capability token carrying that grant to whatever does the work. Pull any one and access stops.

The fourth layer is the one that does the unglamorous work. A capability token is scoped to a specific grant and travels with the request to whatever performs it, so the component doing the reading is carrying its own permission rather than asking a central authority whether it is allowed. Permission moves with the work instead of being looked up beside it.

Default-deny has a cost and it lands on the person, not the machine. Every share becomes an act rather than an assumption, which is more friction than a list-based system. The trade is that nothing is ever shared because somebody forgot to remove a name.

Four layers also means four places to look when access is refused. That is a real cost in confusion, paid to remove a larger cost in surprise.

Trust here is a graph of explicit edges rather than a default you have to remember to remove. A connection is typed — it says what it is for — and the number of close ties is capped at a human figure rather than growing without limit.

The cap is not a technical constraint dressed as a virtue. A trust list that grows past what a person can hold in mind stops being a decision and becomes an accumulation, and an accumulated list is exactly the ambient permission the whole design is trying to replace.

Why every grant is written where it cannot be changed

Every grant and every revocation is written to a hash-chained audit trail, so the history of who could see what, and when, is itself tamper-evident.

Append-only is the first half; hash-chained is the second. One says entries are never removed, the other says earlier entries cannot be changed.

That is a stronger property than logging. A log answers what happened according to whoever kept the log. A hash chain answers what happened according to mathematics, because altering an earlier entry breaks every entry after it.

The reason to want the stronger property is uncomfortable and worth stating. The party most able to quietly rewrite an access record is the party running the system, which in this design is you — and a record you could edit is a record nobody else has reason to accept.

What the trail does not yet do is present itself. The history is recorded and tamper-evident; nothing this week puts it in front of a person as an ordinary thing to read. A record nobody looks at is still worth keeping, but it is not yet doing the job its properties promise.

The default-deny consent engine walks the four layers in order.

What this means, in plain terms

Most of what lands in a week belongs to the thing it landed in. A few things generalise past it. These five are the ones this week actually paid for.

A lock you can be locked out of is a data-loss bug in a costume

Memories are now encrypted field by field, and the work ships with an explicit guarantee that the owner can always reach their own data. Without that clause the same cipher would be a way to permanently destroy a personal archive by accident. Ship the recovery property in the same change as the lock.

Independently revocable layers, or you only have one layer

Access needs identity, a trust edge, a signed consent rule, and a scoped token — and pulling any single one stops it. Layers that can only be removed together are one layer with extra names. A defence you cannot partially withdraw is a defence you will never withdraw.

A record its own keeper can edit is not evidence

Every grant and revocation lands in a hash-chained trail, so changing an old entry breaks the ones after it. The point is not distrust of the operator; it is that a record only persuades when its keeper could not have altered it. Tamper-evidence is what turns a log into evidence.

A migration finishes when the second path is deleted

Moving the data layer meant converting 879 query call sites and stripping raw SQL to zero across the codebase, not merely adding a nicer interface beside the old one. A migration that leaves both paths standing has doubled the maintenance and settled nothing. Removing the old road is the migration.

Cancelling out loud costs less than a half-built feature

Two efforts were cancelled this week — trust decay with demurrage, and standalone revocable access — and the cancellation was recorded rather than left as a stalled item. Silence would have read as work in progress for months. Say when something stops, or the plan quietly becomes fiction.

What changed, area by area

Everything that moved this week, in rough order of how much of it moved. Per-area file counts are not available for this window, so each area is named without one rather than with a figure that cannot be reproduced.

Memory encryption.

Stored memories are encrypted field by field with AES-256-GCM, with an explicit guarantee that the owner can always reach their own data.

The per-field granularity is what keeps a memory searchable while encrypted.

Consent.

Reads and shares are denied by default until a signed grant allows them, across four independently revocable layers, and every grant and revocation is written to a BLAKE3 hash-chained trail that makes later editing detectable.

Trust.

Relationships became explicit typed edges in a graph rather than membership in an ambient list, capped at a human number of close ties.

( Trust is directional .)

Group spaces.

Hives moved from a three-machine local-network proof to working over the open internet, carried by gossip, lazy sync, backup and compaction.

The umbrella effort covering this and the trust work is the Sovereign Foundation.

The data layer.

Every query in the application now goes through one interface, and the database went fully graph-native and the migration finished rather than stalling: 879 query call sites converted, the old graph database removed along with 3,225 lines, and zero raw SQL left in application code.

Zero is the load-bearing part of that sentence: a migration that ends at "almost none" leaves every future reader wondering which calls are the exceptions. ( Killing raw SQL .)

Plugin wiring.

A single plugin-wiring change removed 13,955 lines, the largest deletion of the week.

The unified plugin system it belongs to reached 80 of 80 on its pipeline in a Friday push and closed the day with nine work items still open, so it is progress rather than a finish.

Cross-instance trust.

The first-phase handshake protocol and the friendship keys behind it were started this week using ECDH.

The work did not reach a finish line and is still in progress — started, not finished.

Mobile.

Research for a local daemon on mobile is complete; the first Android milestone is the next step and has not been taken.

Nothing runs on a phone yet.

The Sovereign Foundation.

The umbrella effort covering this week's identity, trust and consent work reached its finished state, which is what makes the four items above a layer rather than four separate features.

It is the connective work: the reason a trust edge, a consent rule and a capability token refer to the same identity.

The two efforts that stopped

Both stopped deliberately, and both are recorded as stopped. Neither is sitting in a backlog reading as work that might still happen, which is the state a quietly abandoned feature normally occupies for a year or more.

Trust decay and demurrage — cancelled.

The idea that trust should erode on its own over time was examined and dropped deliberately.

Standalone revocable access — cancelled.

Revocation survives as a property of the four consent layers rather than as a feature of its own.

What would prove this week's consent claims wrong

Four things this report claims are only half-proven. Each is stated as a commitment rather than a caveat, and each names the evidence that would settle it one way or the other.

  • Default-deny has been built, not yet stress-tested. The four layers exist and each can be pulled. Nothing here shows what happens to a request that nobody anticipated, which is the case the design exists for.
  • The tamper-evident trail is only as good as who reads it. A hash chain makes editing detectable by someone checking. No part of this week makes anyone check.
  • Cross-instance trust is started and unfinished. The handshake's first phase and its key exchange landed. Two machines that have never met still cannot complete the ceremony end to end.
  • The plugin system closed the week with nine open items. A pipeline reading 80 of 80 is not the same statement as the work being done, and the difference is exactly those nine.
