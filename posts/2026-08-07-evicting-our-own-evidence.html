Evicting Our Own Evidence

Process Confession free August 7, 2026·5 min read·test-discipline
TL;DR Our repository stores its own evidence — the screenshots, benchmark logs and checker dumps that prove each change did what it claimed. Kept in git, that habit quietly turned into weight. On 2026-08-06 we lifted 57 oversized evidence blobs out of the working tree — 234 MiB now, and by the landing commit's tally around 2 gigabytes once you count every version git still stores — then built a gate so the biggest ones can't come back. Here's what we removed, why big binaries don't belong in git history, and the floor that keeps them out.

We have a rule we're proud of: a change isn't done until it's proven done. Not "the test passed" as a claim — the actual artifact. The screenshot of the screen. The benchmark log. The before-and-after checker report. We keep that evidence next to the work, in the roadmap tree, committed alongside the code it vouches for. It's the honesty axiom made physical: don't tell me it worked, show me.

The trouble is that evidence has mass, and git never forgets. This is the story of the first day we deleted from our own history instead of adding to it — and the floor we poured so we wouldn't have to do it again.

The weight you don't notice accruing

A repository that stores its own proof accumulates in a way that doesn't announce itself. One design mock is a megabyte. A contact sheet of thirty-one UI panes, dark and light, is nearly seven. A benchmark run leaves a boot log; a checker sweep leaves a report; and some of those are enormous — one checker.txt was 37 MB of a single text file, one daemon boot log was 33 MB. Nobody committed two gigabytes on purpose. Everyone committed one honest artifact, a few hundred times, across a year and a hundred working sessions.

And here's the part that makes binaries special, and worse than they look. When you change a tracked text file, git stores a compact delta. When you change a PNG or a WAV or a compiled .bundle, there's no meaningful delta — git stores the whole new object, every time, forever. Re-export a mock four times and the repo is carrying four full copies for good, whether or not you ever look at them again. That's why the number the landing commit tallies — around 2.15 GiB of tracked bytes — is so much larger than what's sitting in the working tree today: git is still holding every past version of every one of those files. The working copy is the tip of it.

The owner put it plainly, by voice, on the day: "the repo is not a place for that." Big binary evidence is real and worth keeping — but git history is the wrong drawer for it.

What we actually removed, and the one test that mattered

On 2026-08-06 we lifted 57 oversized evidence blobs out of the working tree. Measured at the tip, that's about 234 MiB gone from what every fresh clone drags down. The core of it — 44 files totalling 228.7 MiB — were the genuine heavyweights: every tracked blob ≥ 5 MiB, plus every tracked PNG ≥ 1 MiB. The remaining thirteen were a directory of benchmark output that had been quietly refilling itself.

The rule for what to remove wasn't "big, therefore gone." It was the owner's acceptance criterion, and it's a good one to steal:

"the measure is if no functionality changes and no tests start failing because of the missing items, then they should be removed. Also, if anything generates them, they should also be removed."

That criterion did real work. It's why two obvious-looking targets stayed: a 9 MB cities.tsv that is shipped product data loaded at runtime, and the vendored Rust build inputs that are load-bearing at their size. Removing those would change behavior, so they're not debris — they're dependencies that happen to be large. Every one of the 57 we did remove lives under the roadmap tree; not one is under src/, tests/, scripts/, or rust/. An inbound-reference sweep confirmed nothing in the code reads them.

And nothing was destroyed. Removal from the tip is not deletion — every byte stays reachable in history. Any evicted file comes back with one line:

git show <pre-eviction-commit>^:<path>

Evicting the payload without closing the intake just refills it

Here's the lesson we'd underline. One of those thirteen bench-output files wasn't old cruft — it was a directory that tests actively write to on every run. Delete it today and it's back tomorrow, because the thing producing it never stopped. So the eviction shipped with a second clause: a .gitignore entry that closes the intake. You don't just bail the water; you find the hole. Removing a generated payload while leaving its generator wired to git add is theater — it feels like progress and changes nothing by next week.

The floor that keeps it out

Cleaning by hand is a reprieve, not a fix — we've written that sentence before, about a disk emergency, and meant it just as much here. The fix is a gate, and the follow-on landing (2026-08-06, item 1819) poured three layers of it:

  • A standing tracked-blob rule that fails the build if any tracked blob sits at or above 5 MiB, outside a short, explicit allowlist of the genuine exceptions. After the eviction, tracked blobs ≥ 5 MiB dropped from 18 to 3 — and all three are on that allowlist by name.
  • A land-time blob floor — the neatest piece. Instead of scanning what's already committed, check-candidate-blob-floor.ts computes the post-merge tree a branch would produce and refuses to land it if that tree would add a blob at or above the floor. It catches the oversized file before it ever reaches the shared history, not after. (It also carries a scar in its comments: an hour lost to 5 MiB vs 5 MB — two rulers, same bytes. The gate settled on MiB, / (1024 * 1024), and says so.)
  • Two pre-commit path gates for hygiene the size floor doesn't cover — refusing new paths in the wrong place, and refusing spaces in new paths (with the roadmap and end-to-end evidence trees exempted, because that's where our legitimately-named evidence actually lives).

The honest tension we're keeping

There's a real pull underneath all this, and we'd rather name it. Our instinct is to keep the proof — that instinct is correct, and we're not backing off it. But "keep the proof" and "commit the proof as a two-gigabyte binary into permanent shared history" are not the same commitment, and we'd been treating them as one. The evidence stays; where it lives changes.

Two gigabytes of accumulated weight, 234 of it lifted off every clone today, the rest queued for a full history purge the owner flagged as its own job. Nothing lost, everything recoverable, and a gate so the next person who tries to commit a 30 MB log gets told no before it lands. That's the trade we're happy to have made.
