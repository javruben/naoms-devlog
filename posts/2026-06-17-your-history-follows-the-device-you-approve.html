Your history follows the device you approve

The permission that lets a new device fetch your past is written on the approval path, one step ahead of the message that starts it working

Technology Architect free June 17, 2026·8 min read·identity
TL;DR When you approve a new device, the device you already have writes down the permission to serve your history before it sends the message that starts the new one working. Putting the write ahead of that message is what this change did.

What a new device has to be given

Your identity in NAOMS is not a row in somebody's user table. It is a signed history — an append-only record of everything that has happened to that identity, each entry signed, each one naming the one before it. The history is the account. Anything that wants to act as you has to hold it.

That makes adding a second device a real transfer rather than a login. The new device needs two separate things, and they arrive by two separate routes.

The first is the ability to sign. NAOMS splits the signing key across your devices so that no single one of them holds a whole key, and adding a device means running a short ceremony that produces a fresh share for the newcomer — a piece of the signing power, computed live, rather than a copy of anything the first device already had. That ceremony is its own story, and it builds on the threshold signing underneath.

The second is the history itself, and that is what this piece is about. The device you already own packs its records into one bundle and puts it in a local store that is content-addressed: the bundle's name is a hash of its own bytes, so asking for it by name is asking for exactly those bytes and nothing else. The name travels to the new device on the pairing link. The bytes do not — they are fetched afterwards, over a connection the two devices open between themselves. A relay, a public rendezvous server, is switched on by default and may carry that traffic when the two cannot find a path directly; it helps them meet, and it is part of the picture rather than an exception to it.

The gate in front of your bytes

Serving a bundle to whoever asks would be a poor idea, so the first device does not. Requests for bytes go through a gate with two arms, and both are consulted before anything is sent.

The first arm runs when a connection is accepted. The connecting device's identifier is checked against a membership set, and a device that is not in it is refused there — before it can ask for anything. The second arm runs per request. When a connection asks for a particular bundle, the gate looks up an allow-set: for this device, which bundles may it have? A device with no entry gets a refusal. So does a device with an entry that does not cover the bundle it named.

Both arms are fail-closed, which is the property worth carrying away from this section: the absence of an answer is a refusal. An empty allow-set, a missing entry, a lookup that cannot be resolved — each of those denies. Nothing has to go right for the gate to say no; things have to go right for it to say yes. That is deliberate, and it is why the ordering problem later in this piece showed up as a clean refusal rather than as a hang.

The identifier the gate checks is not something the requesting device asserts in its message. It is read off the connection: the transport's handshake proves that whoever opened it holds the secret key belonging to that public identifier, and the serve loop remembers, per connection, which identifier that was. A device can claim to be anything in a message; the identifier the gate uses is the one the handshake proved possession of.

Two honest notes about the entry that gets written for a newly approved device. It is the broad form — the wildcard that covers every bundle that device is authorized to receive from you, rather than one entry naming the single bundle your history lives in. The reasoning is in the code beside it: a device authorized to read a given history is authorized for that history's bundles, so a per-bundle entry would resolve to the same verdict for each one. A narrower per-bundle form is named there as future work. And the write itself is best-effort. If the transport layer is not up, the call returns without writing anything and without complaining; if it throws, it logs and the pairing carries on regardless. A pairing request that arrives without a network identifier attached — an older peer, say — gets no entry written at all, and the ceremony still proceeds. The code names a second route for a device that ends up without the bundle: a request broadcast over the shared identity channel, answered by whoever holds the history. That route exists. This piece does not claim anything about how it fares.

When the permission arrived after the request

The write described above used to happen somewhere else: in the wrap-up step, after the signing ceremony had committed and the first device was recording the new one as a sibling. That is a reasonable place to record a relationship. It turned out to be the wrong place to record a permission, and the reason is a timing property of the ceremony.

Work out what actually starts the new device fetching. The acceptance message is what sets it going — on seeing it, the new device begins assembling its share. When that assembly completes, the new device resolves its own pairing and moves on to fetching the history. Between the acceptance and the fetch there is no further message from the first device. There used to be a completion frame, and it was removed earlier for unrelated reasons; the new device now finishes on its own work rather than on being told.

So the acceptance is the last thing the first device sends before the new device comes knocking, and everything after it happens on the new device's clock. The wrap-up write sat on the other side of that boundary — the first device was still finishing its own ceremony while the new one was already asking. Two sequences with nothing ordering them.

They resolved in the wrong order, and the failure was quiet in the way fail-closed failures are. The gate had no entry for the asking device, so it refused at once — a fast, well-formed no, not a timeout, not an error anybody would read as a bug. The new device came out of pairing able to sign and with no history to sign about, and the first thing that needed the history failed instead: registering the account found no record of the identity locally.

The repair is an ordering, not a retry

The fix moves the write to the approval path. Once you have approved the device and the pairing code has been re-checked for expiry, the first device writes the serve permission — and only then stamps and broadcasts the acceptance.

That single move is the whole repair, and it works because of the timing property above. The acceptance is what releases the new device to start; a write that happens before the acceptance is a write that happens before anything the new device does in response. The ordering is established by the ceremony's own message sequence rather than by hoping one local step outruns a remote one. The write is a local allow-set entry with nothing to wait on — no network, no agreement with the other side — so it costs nothing to do early, and doing it at the moment of approval reads more honestly anyway: the permission to serve is recorded at the moment the human granted it.

The old write in the wrap-up step is still there, and pushing the same entry twice is harmless. It is now reinforcement rather than the authoritative write.

A guard came with the change. It drives the first device's coordinating component with stand-in parts, records the permission write and the acceptance broadcast into one list, and asserts the write comes strictly first — move the call back after the broadcast and it goes red, which is the mutation the change itself was tested against. A partner check asserts that a pairing request with no network identifier produces no permission write while the acceptance still goes out. Be precise about what that buys: it is an ordering assertion about two calls inside one component, exercised with substitutes for the transport and the signing ceremony. It is not a measurement of a real transfer between two machines, and it does not observe the gate at all.

Which is the honest limit to end on. The change landed on the day this piece is dated, and its own landing note says it had not yet been verified across two hosts — the change is on one side of the language boundary and needed no rebuild of the layer that enforces the gate, so the next step recorded there is to re-run the cross-machine check. Landed is a statement about the code. It is not the same statement as proven, and the two are worth keeping apart.

One thing to carry away as a user rather than as a reader of code. The moment you approve is now the moment authority is granted — earlier than the moment the pairing visibly finishes. A ceremony you start and abandon, because you closed the screen or the other device went away or the code ran out, is still a moment where you said yes to a device. Approving is the decision point. Be sure who is asking there, rather than at the end.
