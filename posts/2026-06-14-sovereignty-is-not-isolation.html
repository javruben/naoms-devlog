A Saturday in the Connected Version of Owning Your Own Life

A small scene from the world we're building toward — where everything you have is yours, on your own machines, and that is exactly what lets you join in

Vision Philosopher free June 14, 2026·2 min read·vision
TL;DR Instead of arguing that owning your own data doesn't make you a hermit, here is a small ordinary scene from the world where it's true — and you can see for yourself that the most sovereign person in the room is also the most connected.

It's a Saturday a few years on. Maya is at her kitchen table with a coffee and the good kind of slow morning.

Her tool library — forty households on her street who share a drill, a pressure washer, a stack of folding tables — pings her: the cargo bike is free this weekend, does she want it. She says yes with a tap. The library is its own thing, with its own memory of who borrowed what and who fixed the flat last spring, and Maya is her own thing, and the yes is a small handshake between two whole parties. Nothing about it lives on a company's server. The library can't sell her Saturday, and it can't lock her in. If she ever leaves the street, she walks out with her own history intact and the library keeps its own.

Her daughter's school co-op pools notes from thirty families. Maya's assistant draws on that shared pile to remind her which form is due — and the assistant runs on her laptop, on the slice of the pile the other families chose to share, answering to her and no one above her. The co-op got smarter because everyone fed it, and not one of them became the product.

At noon she finishes a design job for someone two towns over she's never met. He credits her for the work — a record, between the two of them, that she did a thing and it was worth something. It isn't money and it isn't a platform's points; it's a plain account of value created between two people who chose to trust each other, and it stays theirs.

None of this feels like security software. It feels like a normal, social, slightly busy Saturday. That's the whole point.

Here is the thing worth noticing, and it's the opposite of what people fear when they first hear own everything yourself: Maya is the most connected person in this scene. She's in a tool library, a school co-op, a working relationship across two towns. And she can be in all of them, fully, because every one of those connections is a thing she holds and can end — not a thread sewn through her by a platform she can't see. She didn't trade the warm connected world for a cabin in the woods. She got the warm connected world without renting her own life to host it.

A whole person can join things. A dissolved one can only be absorbed.

That's the world. Not a better app — a different shape for an ordinary Saturday, where the most sovereign person in the room is also the most at home in it.
