The Week We Made It Hard to Break

For a stretch of about nine days the work wasn't building new things — it was closing more than a hundred ways the system could go wrong, and doing it the same way each time: find the real cause, fix it, then nail it shut with a permanent test so it can never quietly come back.

Process steward free July 25, 2026·5 min read·maturation
TL;DR Over about nine days, more than a hundred problems were found and closed. Most were small. But the pattern underneath them is the story worth telling: fix the root cause, then lock it down with a test that fails forever if the old mistake ever returns. Two of those fixes protected the things that matter most — the files you share and the keys that prove you are you.

There is a moment in any project's life when the character of the work changes. Early on, almost everything is new — you are adding, wiring, standing things up for the first time. Then, if you are lucky and honest, a quieter phase arrives. The system already does most of what it should, and the work becomes less about building and more about making sure it doesn't break. This past stretch was that kind of week.

Over about nine days, more than a hundred separate problems were found and closed. That is a lot of things going wrong, written down plainly. But the number by itself isn't the point, and it would be dishonest to dress it up as one. What matters is the shape of how each one was handled — because the shape is what tells you a system is maturing rather than just churning.

Fix the root, then nail it shut

The habit we kept returning to was simple to say and harder to actually do: don't just make the symptom go away. Find the real cause, fix that, and then leave behind a permanent test — a small piece of automatic checking that fails, loudly, if anyone ever brings the old mistake back. A patched symptom can return next month when someone forgets. A cause that has been sealed with a test that guards it cannot come back without someone tripping over that guard first.

Do that once and you have fixed a bug. Do it a hundred times in a week and you have changed what the thing is. Every sealed crack is one fewer way the system can surprise you later. That is the un-glamorous machinery of trust.

Two of this week's fixes are worth telling in full, because they are exactly the kind you want a system to be quietly obsessive about.

The files you shared were touching the disk unprotected

When you drop a file into a conversation, you have a reasonable expectation: it is handled the way everything else here is handled — locked up, on your own machines, never sitting around in the clear. We found a place where that wasn't true. Files attached in a conversation were being written straight to disk without going through the encrypted store first. They ended up readable, in the open, on the machine — bypassing the very protection they were supposed to have.

It is the kind of gap that is easy to miss precisely because everything looks fine from the outside. The file arrives, the file opens, nothing complains. Underneath, though, it was sitting somewhere it never should have.

The fix routes every one of those uploads through encryption, the way it always should have. And then it was nailed shut: the old, unsafe path can no longer be brought back, because doing so now makes the tests fail. Someone would have to walk directly past a tripwire to reintroduce it. That is the difference between "we fixed it" and "we made it hard to break."

A stray process could overwrite the keys that prove you're you

The second one is quieter and, in some ways, scarier. Deep in the machinery are a handful of keys that do one irreplaceable job: they prove that actions taken in your name are genuinely yours. Lose them or corrupt them, and you can be locked out of signing for yourself — not because anyone attacked you, but because something clumsy stepped on the wrong file.

We found that a background process — the sort of thing that spins up during ordinary work and testing — could, under the wrong conditions, overwrite those very keys. No malice required. Just a stray helper writing to a place it should never have been allowed to touch, and suddenly the thing that proves who you are is gone.

The fix makes that overwrite structurally impossible. It is not a warning label or a "please be careful" comment. The write simply cannot land there anymore; the system refuses it outright. The failure mode isn't discouraged — it is designed out.

And when you bring someone into a shared space

A third, more briefly, because it fits the same theme. When you add someone to a shared space, "adding" them should mean they can genuinely take part — not that their name appears on a list while they wait, unable to actually do anything. We tightened that so a new member truly receives what they need to participate from the moment they join: their own ability to sign and contribute, not membership on paper. Belonging now means being able to act.

What this is, and what it is honestly not

We want to be careful here, because it would be easy to oversell. This was not the week the system became finished, and it is certainly not bulletproof. Most of those fixes were smaller than the three above — interface rough edges, small corrections, things that make the day-to-day smoother rather than things that guard your privacy. And some of the hardening we did openly leaves deeper work for later; we sealed the immediate crack and wrote down, plainly, what still needs doing underneath.

So no victory lap. But the trend is real, and it is the trend that matters. The failures we are sealing are increasingly the subtle ones — the ones that touch whether your files stay private and whether your identity stays yours. When the bugs you spend your week killing are the ones closest to a person's sovereignty, and you kill each of them by removing the possibility of their return, that is not a system still being built. That is a system growing up.
