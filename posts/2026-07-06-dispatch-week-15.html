Four things changed this week

One per area. Behind last week's new doorways, the work itself moved onto your hardware.

1

Your photos are indexed by a model on your own machine

A vision model turns each photo into a point in a space of visual meaning, so you can find one by what is in it rather than by its filename or its date.

2

Value can move between two people and cannot half-finish

A transfer needs both devices to co-sign, so it cannot be recorded as sent on one side without being received on the other. Unreachable means refused.

3

A group call's encryption carries your consent inside it

Every media frame is signed over the encryption epoch and the version of your consent state, so a peer cannot swap your consent stamp or replay an old key.

4

One pipeline builds for Mac, Linux and Android

The same core produces a self-contained daemon on three operating systems, packaged differently on each because each one demands it.

Finding a photo by what is in it

The feature: searching your own photo library by content — showing it a picture and getting the ones that look like it.

Before: a photo could be found by its filename, its date, or a folder somebody had put it in, which is a search of the labels rather than the images.

Now: every photo becomes a point in a 1,024-dimension space of visual meaning, and "find photos like this one" is a real nearest-neighbour search in that space, fused with text pulled out of the image by on-device character recognition.

The model runs on your machine. Your pictures are not sent anywhere to be understood, which is the whole reason this was worth the extra work rather than a call to a hosted service that would have returned tags a week sooner.

The honest edge, stated where you will see it. Today this is image-to-image: show it a photo and it finds similar ones. Typing a description for the model to match — text to image — is the next step, and it is not this one.

Two more models that stayed home

Live speech transcription is being built out of a model that has no streaming mode at all. The engine decodes a fixed window and forgets everything between calls, so we own a sliding buffer on our side and stitch overlapping decodes into a running caption.

The raw audio is discarded the instant it is used — a rule we can hold because the decoding happens here. It is an in-progress path rather than a finished feature, and the mechanism is real.

Group calls are being encrypted with modern group cryptography, with consent folded into the packets themselves. The encryption epoch and the consent-state version are both covered by each frame's signature.

That is what makes the consent unforgeable rather than merely recorded: a peer who alters your consent stamp, or replays a key from earlier in the call, breaks the signature and is caught by the arithmetic rather than by a policy.

The claim we took back and then earned

The feature: sending value to another person, so it lands in their balance on their device.

Before: the claim had been made once, in June, and was retracted when its proof did not survive a first-hand look.

Now: it is remade on a proof that did — every transfer co-signed by both devices, and refused outright rather than left half-done when the other side cannot be reached.

What moves is not money and not a currency. It is a shared record of mutual obligation — a two-person IOU, kept honestly on both sides, with no bank, no central ledger and no global blockchain underneath it.

A transfer that can half-finish is worse than one that fails. Because both devices sign, there is no state in which one side has recorded a payment sent and the other has not recorded it received. The two records are the same record.

Why the retraction is the interesting part

The most consequential engineering event of the week added no feature. A flagship claim was pulled in June because the evidence behind it did not hold up to direct inspection, and was not allowed back until it did.

A project whose premise is honesty does not get to be honest only when honesty is cheap. The mechanism that keeps the rest of these claims worth reading is exactly this one: the willingness to un-ship a sentence.

A version number should mean proven, not promised

The value claim was published in June, retracted when its proof did not survive a direct look, and republished in July on evidence that did. The retraction cost a headline and bought the only thing that makes the other headlines worth reading. A claim you are unwilling to withdraw is not a claim; it is a position.

Refuse rather than half-finish

A value transfer needs both devices to co-sign, and is refused outright when the other device cannot be reached. The tempting alternative — record it here, sort the other side out later — always works, and produces two records that disagree. Where a partial result is indistinguishable from a whole one, refuse instead.

Bind a permission to the thing it governs

Consent in a group call is not stored beside the media; the consent-state version is inside what each frame's signature covers. A peer who swaps it breaks the signature. A permission kept next to the data it protects can be edited separately from it — fold it in, and tampering becomes arithmetic.

A guarantee you cannot inspect is a policy

Speech is transcribed on your machine so we can promise the raw audio is discarded the instant it is used. A hosted transcription service can state the same intention and cannot let you verify it. Prefer the guarantee whose enforcement is visible to the person it protects over the one that is asserted.

Only a machine that has never seen your source can test a fresh install

An outside developer can now build a package with no copy of our source on their machine. We found the five walls in the way by walking a clean machine into each of them, one at a time. A developer experience tested on a developer's own machine is tested on the one configuration that cannot fail.

Builds and packaging.

One pipeline now produces Mac, Linux and Android builds, each running its own self-contained daemon off the same core.

The same native code has to be packaged differently on each system — statically folded in on Mac, its whole shared-library closure staged and re-verified on Linux, aligned to 16-kilobyte memory pages on Android. Only the Mac build is finished, and only for macOS; Linux ships a daemon archive rather than a double-click application; Android runs on-device under active hardening. Onboarding.

The redesigned first run is invitation-first and built around how each step should feel, with the 24-word recovery phrase made something you can defer rather than a wall you are trapped behind, and your recommended local model downloading quietly in the background while you finish setting up.

It has landed and is still in progress.

The developer kit.

An outside developer can now build a NAOMS package with none of our source on their machine — scaffolding, signing, publishing and promoting into a shared space through the installed command-line tool, with a real countersignature at the trust boundary.

What v0.6 has proven, and the four places it has not

v0.6 is not the week we added the most. It is the week the thinking and the keys moved onto your device, each piece shipped with its limits written beside it. Four of those limits are the ones that would settle the release's claims.

  • A vision model that cannot be asked a question. Photo search matches an image against images. Until a typed description reaches the same index, the feature is a lookalike finder rather than a search.
  • Two platforms out of three are unfinished. Only macOS has a completed build. A Linux daemon archive and an Android build under hardening are real and are not the same claim as "installs on three platforms."
  • Encrypted media is not a calling feature. The consent binding and the group cryptography are verified. Nobody has yet held a multi-party call on top of them.
  • Value has only ever moved between two devices. The co-signing is what makes it honest and is also what bounds it. Nothing here shows value reaching a third person.
