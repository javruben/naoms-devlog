Dispatch · Week 15: The Intelligence Moved On-Device

The week a vision model started indexing your photos on your own machine, speech transcription and group-call encryption ran locally, value learned to move without a middleman, and the whole daemon shipped self-contained on Mac, Linux, and Android. It's the release that closes v0.6 — and the honest part is how carefully we marked what's finished versus what's still being built.

Process Dispatch free July 6, 2026·7 min read·meta
TL;DR Week 14 moved NAOMS out of the terminal and into apps you open. Week 15 moved the hard part — the intelligence and the keys — onto your own device. A vision model now indexes your photos locally so you can find one by what's in it; speech transcription and group-call encryption run on your machine, not a server; value moves between two people and refuses to half-finish; and the whole self-contained daemon now builds for Mac, Linux, and Android from one pipeline. It's the release that closes v0.6. What makes it a release and not a demo is the discipline underneath: every claim marked for exactly how far it goes, one headline retracted and re-earned before we kept it, and the parts that aren't done named plainly rather than implied finished.

Last week the story was doorways: NAOMS became a thing you open — a window, a phone, a voice. The obvious next question is the one that actually earns a version number. Behind those doorways, where does the work happen — the understanding, the encryption, the keys? For a lot of software the honest answer is "a server we run." This week, across a run of separate landings that all point the same way, the answer became: your own device.

A vision model that indexes your photos runs on your machine. Speech transcription runs on your machine. The cryptography that protects a group call runs on your machine. The keys that move value are co-signed between two people's devices with nothing in the middle. And the whole self-contained daemon that hosts all of it now builds for three operating systems from one pipeline. That is the spine of v0.6: not new doorways, but the intelligence and the secrets moving off the server and onto the hardware in your hands.

The most important thing that happened isn't a line added. It's a claim taken back until it was true.

Your device got smart about your photos

The flagship. Your photo library is now indexed by a real vision transformer — running on your own device, so you can find a photo by what's actually in it, not by its filename or date. Every photo becomes a point in a 1024-dimension space of visual meaning; "find photos like this one" is a genuine nearest-neighbor search in that space, fused with text pulled out of the image by on-device OCR. The model never sends your pictures anywhere — the understanding happens on your hardware. The honest edge, stated in the piece: today it's image-to-image (show it a photo, it finds similar ones); typing a description for the vision model to match — text-to-image — is the next step, not this one.

Two more models, running locally

The same "on your device, not a server" instinct carried two harder pieces.

We're building live speech transcription out of a model that has no streaming mode at all. The on-device speech engine only decodes a fixed window and forgets everything between calls — so we own a sliding-window buffer on the host side and stitch overlapping decodes into a live caption stream, with a hard rule that the raw audio is gone the instant it's used. It's an in-progress path, not a finished feature, and we say so — but the mechanism is real and the reason to do it the hard way is exactly that a cloud transcription service can't promise your audio never lingers.

And group calls are being encrypted with real modern group cryptography, with consent bound into the packets themselves. Every media frame carries the encryption epoch and a consent-state version folded into its signature, so a peer can't silently swap your consent stamp or replay an old key mid-stream without breaking the signature. The crypto is wired and verified; full multi-party calling is still an open rung, and the piece is plain about that line.

Value that can't half-finish

You can now send value to another person and it lands in their balance on their device — no bank, no central ledger, no global blockchain. It isn't money and it isn't a currency; it's a shared record of mutual obligation, a two-person IOU kept honestly on both sides. Every transfer needs both devices to co-sign, so it can't be recorded as sent on one side without being received on the other; and if the other device can't be reached, the transfer is refused rather than left half-done. This is the claim that was retracted in June and re-earned in July — the whole story is in the article, because a claim about value has to be earned, not asserted.

One memory system, three platforms

All of that on-device work needs to actually install on real machines. This week one build pipeline produces Mac, Linux, and Android builds, each running its own self-contained daemon off the same core — and the genuinely interesting engineering is that the same native code has to be packaged differently on each operating system (statically folded in on Mac, its whole shared-library closure staged and re-verified on Linux, aligned to new 16-kilobyte memory pages on Android). The honest scope matters here: only the Mac build is celebrated, and only for macOS; Linux ships a self-contained daemon tarball, not yet a double-click desktop app; Android runs on-device but is under active hardening. "One pipeline, three platforms" is true; "the same polished app on all three" is not, and we don't pretend otherwise.

A first run that earns your trust

The way in got rethought, too. The redesigned onboarding flow is now live on the main line: invitation-first, designed around how each step should feel, with the 24-word recovery phrase made something you can defer instead of a wall you're trapped behind, and your recommended local model downloading quietly in the background while you finish setting up. It's live-on-main but still an in-progress effort, not a celebrated milestone.

And for the developers

One more, for the people who want to build on this. An outside developer can now build a NAOMS package with none of our source on their machine — scaffold, sign, publish, and promote into a shared space through the installed command-line tool, with a real countersignature at the trust boundary. We found the five walls the honest way, by walking a clean machine into each of them. Two quieter reliability landings round out the week: imports from Google Drive and Gmail that re-prove they reach your data every run, and a revocation leak sealed so a just-revoked app stops being handed fresh keys.

The honest ledger

Told straight, by status:

  • Shipped and celebrated (close-gate signed): peer-to-peer value movement; the SDK proven usable by an outside developer; the on-device photo search experience; the notarized macOS desktop app.
  • Live on main, honestly in-progress: the photo vision-embedding foundation; on-device speech transcription; group-media encryption; the Linux self-contained daemon build; the Android on-device app; the redesigned onboarding flow; real-account imports; the revocation-leak fix.
  • Named, not claimed: text-to-image photo search; full multi-party group calling; a Linux double-click desktop app; the unified connect-friends-and- devices redesign (not yet merged); value that circulates beyond two people.

We mark these on purpose. Celebrate what's real; never imply the partial is finished.

v0.6

This is the release that closes here. v0.6 isn't "the week we added the most." It is the week the thinking and the keys moved onto your device — a model that understands your photos, speech and call encryption that run locally, value co- signed between two people, and a self-contained daemon that installs on three platforms — each shipped with its limits written next to it, and one headline re-earned only after we were willing to un-ship it. The measure of v0.6 isn't how much we claimed. It's how much of it runs on your own machine, and how honestly we drew the line around the parts that don't yet.

From the workshop

The Server You Didn't Have to Trust

Count how many of this week's landings quietly refuse the same shortcut. The photo model could have run in a cloud and returned tags; it runs on your device. Speech could have been a hosted transcription API; it's local, and the audio is shredded as it's used. Value could have settled through a ledger everyone defers to; it's co-signed between two people with nothing in the middle. Every one of those was the harder path, chosen because the moment the intelligence or the keys live on someone else's computer, "your memory, your device" has a hole in it no interface can cover. v0.6 is the release where we kept paying that cost on purpose.

From the workshop

The Claim You're Willing to Retract

The most important engineering event of the week wasn't a commit — it was a retraction, a value claim pulled in June because its proof didn't hold, and not allowed back until it did. A system whose whole premise is honesty doesn't get to be shy about the one mechanism that keeps the honesty real: the willingness to take a claim back. That's why this dispatch spends as much ink on what isn't finished — text-to-image search, full group calling, a Linux desktop app — as on what is. A version number should mean this was proven, not this was promised.
