The Plane Where Three Things Finally Fit in One Bag

A small scene from the world we're building toward — where software that lives on your own machine, an intelligence small enough to come home, and a plain wish to own your own life all turn out to be in the same place at the same time

Vision Philosopher free June 14, 2026·2 min read·vision
TL;DR Instead of arguing that local-first software, personal AI, and a hunger for sovereignty have converged into the moment worth building in, here is a small ordinary scene where all three are simply in the same room at once — and you can see why it had to be now.

It's a few years on, and Theo is at thirty thousand feet with the wifi off, working the way he prefers to work — alone with his own machine and no signal at all.

His laptop has everything: the notes, the half-written proposal, the years of memory he's kept. None of it is "in the cloud" waiting for a connection. It's just here, on the device on the tray table, syncing with his phone in his pocket and nothing else. The plane could fly forever and he'd lose nothing, because nothing was ever borrowed from a data center on the ground.

He asks the assistant on his laptop to pull together what he promised the client last month. It answers in a second — reading his own memory, running right there on his own hardware, speaking only to him. The question never left the plane. There's no informant on the other end of a wire, because there's no wire. A few years ago an assistant this useful meant shipping your most private thoughts to someone else's machine to be read on the way. Now it fits in the bag.

And the reason Theo lives this way isn't that someone sold it to him. It's that he got tired — tired of services that changed the rules overnight, tired of finding out his private things were never private, tired of building a decade of memory inside something that could vanish or sour or sell. He went looking for the version where his life is his. He found it because, for the first time, it was actually there to find.

He closes the laptop as the plane begins its descent, the work done, the wifi still off.

Here's what the scene quietly reveals. For years these were three separate things, each useless without the others. Software on your own machine, without an intelligence to use it, was a quieter notebook. An intelligence that still had to phone home wasn't private at all. And the plain wish to own your own life, without the tools to make it real, was just frustration. Theo's tray table is the moment all three turned out to be in the same place — the home, the intelligence that can live in it, and the person who actually wanted both. That's the only reason any of this is possible now and wasn't a few years ago.

An honest word: not all of Theo's flight exists yet. The home for your data on your own machines, and an assistant that runs locally and answers only to you, are the direction we're walking, one verifiable step at a time. We'd rather you trust the parts that fly than believe in a finished thing that isn't built. The window where the tools, the models, and the will all line up is open now, and won't stay open forever. The whole project is a wager on laying the floor while it is.
