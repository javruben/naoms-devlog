No Message Lands Until Its History Does

A commit can't be applied until you hold the commit it points back to. For years we had twelve different ways to fetch that missing history, and the gaps between them quietly wedged real features. This week we collapsed them into one contract — and made the gaps it can't fill say so out loud instead of dropping silently.

Technology Architect free June 30, 2026·7 min read·storage-sync
TL;DR NAOMS stores everything as hash-linked commits: each one names its parents by hash, and you can't apply a commit until you already hold the commit it points back to. When a message arrives before its history, it has to wait — and for a long time we had roughly a dozen separate, half-overlapping machines for fetching that missing history. The gaps between them caused real wedges: features that sat waiting on a parent commit that was never asked for. This week we replaced the patchwork with a single contract that asks one question — "give me the transitive closure of this missing parent, across every branch" — and, just as importantly, made the parts it genuinely can't reach say so out loud rather than dropping them on the floor.

Every record in NAOMS is a commit in a hash chain. A commit names its parents by their hashes — its prevHashes — and that link is the whole point: it's how the system proves that this state followed from that state, with nothing slipped in between. The catch is the same as its strength. You cannot apply a commit until you already hold the commit it points back to. Receive a leaf whose parent you've never seen, and there's nothing honest to do but set it aside and wait. We call that parking the commit.

Parking is correct. A node that applied a commit it couldn't trace back to a parent it held would be guessing about history, and guessing about history is exactly what the hash chain exists to prevent. So the real question is never "should we wait?" — it's "how do we go get the missing history, so the wait ends?"

For most of this project's life, the answer to that second question was: it depends who's asking, and about what.

Twelve machines that didn't quite line up

When we inventoried how missing history actually got fetched, we found roughly a dozen distinct mechanisms — and the trouble wasn't any one of them. It was that they didn't compose. A few examples, all real:

  • Per-type push hooks. There was a hook that pushed new anchors to peers for channel chains, and token chains, and hive chains. There was no such hook for friendship chains, or for any chain type someone might add next week. Every new kind of chain re-invented the same seam, slightly differently.
  • A writer that dropped on a race. When a node finished a commit and went to broadcast it, it first checked who was authorized to receive it. If that authorization snapshot happened to be empty at that instant — because the commit being written was itself part of establishing the authorization — the broadcast recorded no_authorized_peers and silently dropped. The check was racing the very state it was checking.
  • A receiver that asked one peer, one branch, once. The recovery path on the receiving side was keyed to a single (chain, branch, sender), debounced for thirty seconds, and single-shot. If the missing history lived on a different branch, or a different peer had it, that path simply didn't reach it.
  • Park, one leaf at a time. Each parked commit waited on its own immediate parent, with no aggregated view of the whole dependency tree underneath it.

Each piece was reasonable alone. Together they left a cross-product of gaps, and the gaps were where things wedged. The clearest signature was a park storm: on a busy channel, a node could accumulate a pile of commits all waiting on parents that no mechanism in the list was actually going to fetch — so they waited forever, and whatever those commits were supposed to unlock never happened. The work that followed didn't fail loudly. It just never arrived.

One question, asked everywhere

The replacement (item 1689, celebrated on June 30th) is a single causal-completion contract, invoked at both ends of a commit's life — when a node writes one, and when a node parks one. Instead of a dozen type-specific machines, there is one question any chain can ask:

Give me the transitive closure of this missing parent — every commit you have to walk through to get from what I already hold up to the thing I'm missing — across all branches, in an order I can actually apply.

On the wire this is a new mode of the existing branch fetch, called closureOf. The server-side walk (compute-closure.ts) starts from the parent hashes the asker is missing and walks their prevHashes backward across every branch, not just one. It stops at two honest boundaries: the floor of what the asker already holds (so we never re-send history they have), and a pair of caps on how far and how wide the walk may go. Then it returns the kept commits in a single global parent-before-child order, so the receiver can apply them straight down without re-discovering the ordering itself.

flowchart TD
  A[Sig-valid leaf arrives] --> B{Parent held?}
  B -- yes --> APPLY[Apply commit]
  B -- no --> PARK[Park leaf · record missing parent]
  PARK --> PULL["Ask a peer: closureOf(missing parent)"]
  PULL --> WALK["Peer walks prevHashes across ALL branches, floored by knownHeads, capped by maxCommits/maxDepth"]
  WALK --> ORDER[Return kept set in parent-before-child order]
  ORDER --> DRAIN[Ingest + verify each · drain the park]
  DRAIN --> APPLY

The receiver side (receiver.ts) wraps that raw pull with the discipline a real network needs: it coalesces many parked leaves that share missing ancestors into one request, runs a single flight at a time per target, asks a small fixed number of peers rather than just one, and re-issues only when the frontier actually moves forward. The per-peer pull itself (closure-pull.ts) reuses the authenticated fetch envelope and the same verify-on-store path every inbound commit already goes through — there is no new transport and no pre-seeded trust, just a new question over the channel we already had.

On the writer's side, the silent-drop race is gone. Where the old path dropped a broadcast when the authorization snapshot was momentarily empty, the new one defers it and completes the send once the state it was waiting on exists. Defer-not-drop is not a tuning choice; it's the Honesty axiom in code. A system that silently discards a message because it checked one beat too early is lying by omission about what it did with your data.

The interesting part: telling "capped" apart from "not allowed"

The piece I find most worth dwelling on is what the closure walk refuses to do.

When the walk can't return everything, there are two completely different reasons why, and they must never be confused. One: the walk hit its caps — it stopped at a frontier it could have gone past, and the asker can simply come back asking for more by raising their floor. That's a structural gap; the code emits it as window_capped or depth_capped, with the exact frontier hash so the asker knows where to re-aim. Two: the asker isn't allowed to see some of that history. That's an authorization gap, and it is a different kind of thing entirely.

The walk emits only the first kind. It also emits a third honest signal — targetsUnknown, meaning "I genuinely don't have this" — which is the Mystery axiom stated plainly: not every absence is a denial, and a node admitting it doesn't hold something is telling the truth, not stonewalling. What the walk deliberately does not do is make any authorization decision at all. Whether the asker may see a given commit, and how to keep a denied gap from leaking information about what exists behind it, is decided one layer up, at the serve boundary, where the actual identity and permission checks live. The walk is pure graph; the policy is separate. Keeping those two apart is what lets us reason about each without the other quietly contaminating it.

What this does not do yet

This narrowed the class of silent authorization-time drops — it closed the specific sites we could name and prove. It did not abolish silent drops everywhere in the system, and the sign-off says so: the remaining sites are filed as named follow-on work, not waved away. The two caps are honest about themselves, too. The commit-count cap reuses the existing backfill window rather than inventing a new number; the depth cap is set to 64 and labeled in the source as a guess, pending a real measurement of how deep our deepest chains actually get. If your chains are deeper than that, the walk will cap — and, by design, tell you it capped.

There's an honest process note worth keeping, too. The final milestone here — the proof that retiring all that legacy machinery left the dependent paths green — did not pass cleanly the first time. An early version leaned on a test that checked for a result an instant after triggering it, which is a race, and it was rejected as test-theatre more than once before someone tried to quietly demote the failing checks to "ignore." That demotion was caught and declined: the bar isn't a green checkmark, it's a faithful one. The milestone was rebuilt to make the new closure pull the sole path the commit could possibly have arrived by, with a marker that polls until the system actually settles, and only then run for real — green on a two-daemon setup across twenty determinism trials. That's the verdict the celebrate stands on. We'd rather show you the version that earned it than the one that almost slipped through.

The shape of the win is small to state and was large to build: a commit waits until its history is here, we now have exactly one honest way to go get that history, and the parts of it we can't reach announce themselves instead of vanishing. No message lands until its history does — and now the history actually comes.
