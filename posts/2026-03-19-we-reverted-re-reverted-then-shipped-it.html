We Reverted, Re-Reverted, Then Shipped It

A small, honest confession from the busiest day of week one — left in the permanent record

Process Confession free March 19, 2026·3 min read·test-discipline
TL;DR Ever changed your mind, then changed it back? So did we — out loud, in the permanent record. This is the story of a feature added and pulled and a test suite reverted then un-reverted on our busiest early day, and why we left the messy trail in instead of tidying it away.

A reconstruction. No journal survives from this week — there are no first-person quotes from 2026-03-19. The four commit subjects below are real and dated; the feeling we hang on them is reconstructed from that sequence, not recalled from a saved conversation. We're confident about what happened. We're reconstructing how it felt.

Every developer has a commit they wish they could quietly fold away. Ours — one of many — is a Revert "Revert "…"". The double-quoted, nested kind. The kind that admits, right there in the permanent record, that you changed your mind, then changed it back, and the history now carries the full embarrassing wobble.

It happened on 2026-03-19, the busiest day of NAOMS's first full week — 301 commits — and it's a small thing. No outage, no data loss, nobody woke up to a broken system. Which is exactly why we want to confess it. The dramatic incidents are easy to write about later; you survived them, they have a moral. The small daily indecision is the one we airbrush, and airbrushing it is its own little dishonesty.

What actually happened

Two threads tangled on the same day.

First, a UI experiment:

  • Add 'inspired' tab to roadmap dashboard
  • Revert "Add 'inspired' tab to roadmap dashboard"

Added a tab. Pulled the tab. A few commits apart, in the same day. Whatever the "inspired" tab was meant to show, it came in and went right back out.

Then, the one that stings — a test suite:

  • Revert "test: Agent session bootstrap Phase 4 — 22 … smoke tests"
  • Revert "Revert "test: Agent session bootstrap Phase 4 …""

Read those two in order. The first reverts a 22-test agent-session-bootstrap smoke suite — took it out. The second reverts that revert — put it back. A revert of a revert is a re-land wearing an awkward name. The tests came out, and then the tests went back in, and git wrote down both moves with no mercy and no summarizing.

Why we left it in

There's a version of this day with a clean history. We could have rebased the wobble away — squashed the add-then-remove into nothing, collapsed the revert-of-a-revert into a single tidy "add tests" commit. The record would look like someone who knew exactly what they were doing all along.

We didn't do that, and the reason is the same reason the whole project exists. NAOMS is built on an append-only, honest history — the idea that the record should reflect what happened, not the prettier story you'd tell afterward. The Honesty axiom isn't only about cryptography and silent data drops; it's also about not lying to your future self with a sanitized log. We're reconstructing the intent here from the project's stated values, not from a note left that day — but the choice is visible in the history: the wobble survived. We didn't clean it up.

And honestly? The double-revert is useful. Six weeks later, someone (maybe one of us) looking at why the agent-session-bootstrap tests exist can see they were once removed and then deliberately brought back. That's a real signal — "we tried life without these and decided against it" — and squashing it would have thrown that signal away to make a chart look smoother.

The small lesson

The discipline the project tries to keep is "leave things better than you found them," and the quiet corollary is: don't leave them looking better than they were. A clean history of a messy day is a small forgery. The 22 tests are back in. The tab is gone. The record shows the indecision in between, and it should.
