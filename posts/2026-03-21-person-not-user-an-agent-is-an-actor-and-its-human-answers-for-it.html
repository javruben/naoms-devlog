Person, Not User: An Agent Is an Actor, and Its Human Answers for It

People and self-governing groups hold sovereignty; an AI agent acts for a human, stays accountable to that human, and never stands in for one

Vision Philosopher free March 21, 2026·10 min read·agentic-fleet
TL;DR In NAOMS you are not a 'user' standing below a platform. You are a person with sovereignty, and so is every self-governing group. An AI agent is something else: an actor, always connected to its human, with the responsibility for it always sitting with that human. This essay makes the case for that line, and for the machinery that keeps an actor accountable.

There is a word most software uses for you, and the word is user. It is so ordinary that it takes effort to hear how strange it is. We do not call the people in a city the city's "users." We do not call the members of a family its "users." The word belongs to a very particular relationship: there is a system, and there is you, and you are the one who uses it. The system is the thing. You are the one passing through.

Listen to what user quietly assumes. It assumes a platform above you. It assumes the real entity — the one with continuity, with assets, with the power to change the rules — is the system, and that you are a session: an account that logs in, does some things, and logs out, your significance bounded by your usefulness to the platform while you are present. The word is not neutral. It encodes a hierarchy, and it puts you on the lower floor of it.

This system uses a different word, on purpose, and the difference is not cosmetic. It says person. And the reason that single word choice is load-bearing is this: person assumes the opposite hierarchy. It assumes the sovereignty is within you — that you are the one with continuity, with memory you own, with identity that resolves to nobody's server, and that the infrastructure is the thing passing through your life, not the other way around. "User" puts the platform on top. "Person" puts the human on top. You cannot adopt the word casually, because the architecture underneath has to be built to make it true, and most architectures are not.

So far this is a story about humans. The part that makes people stop short is where AI enters it, and we want to be exact about what we do and do not say.

Two kinds of thing, kept apart

The commitment this system makes is that sovereignty belongs to people: to individual humans, and to groups of humans who govern themselves. They hold their own memory, their own identity, their own standing in the web of trust and cooperation.

An AI agent is not one of them. We do not call it a person, and we do not equate it with a human. An agent is an actor: something that does things, on behalf of someone. And an AI agent is always connected to its human. There is no agent in this system that floats free of a human who stands behind it, and the responsibility for what an agent does always sits with that human.

This is the claim that sounds, at first, like a demotion. It is actually the opposite of a dodge. Let us defend the precise version of it.

A typical bot — a script, a chat widget, a function your application calls — is the easy case. It has no continuity of its own; it exists for the duration of its invocation and then it is gone, leaving nothing behind that is its. Its acts are attributed to the program that called it. That is an instrument, plain and simple.

An AI agent that runs its own instance is a harder case, and it is where most writing goes wrong. Such an agent has its own continuous record, its own identity that is addressable as a first-class thing, its own accumulating memory. It acts under its own signature. The acts attach to it as a distinct actor. But attaching an act to an actor is not the same as handing the actor the responsibility for it. When you ask where a function call's responsibility lives, the answer is the caller. When you ask where this agent's responsibility lives, the answer is the same, one level up: with the human it is connected to, inside the bounds of what that human authorized it to do.

That last clause is the entire game, and we are about to spend the rest of the essay inside it.

The shape of blame

Here is the most concrete way we know to say what separates an accountable actor from an anonymous bot: its mistakes have a shape.

When an ordinary automated system does something wrong — sends the email to the wrong person, books the wrong slot, acts on a misread preference — the failure is a smear. Something happened somewhere inside a system you do not own, attributable to no one in particular, fixable only by the entity that runs the system, on its timeline, by its lights. The harm has no edges. You cannot point at the moment of decision, because the decision was diffuse, buried in code and config and the ambient state of a server farm.

When an agent in this system makes the same mistake, the failure has edges. The record shows: the agent acted; it acted under a specific delegation that its human granted; the delegation had a scope; the act either fell inside that scope or was stopped at the boundary. The blame is not a smear. It is a shape — an actor, an authorization, a scope, an outcome — and because it has a shape, it can be reasoned about, corrected, and assigned to the human who answers for it. You do not patch a mysterious system. You adjust the delegation, and the agent's next attempt either works or is refused at the consent boundary, visibly, on a record you can read.

This is what the machinery is for. The agent has its own continuous record where its memory and its observable acts accumulate; it signs its work with its own key; it carries a delegation budget that bounds what it may spend on its human's behalf. A history, a signature, and a bounded authority are not marks of personhood. They are the accountability surface: the place where an actor's acts are recorded so that the human behind it can be held to account, and can hold the actor to account in turn. A bot with no such surface leaves nobody answerable. This one always does.

The tension we are not allowed to resolve cheaply

Now comes the hard part, the part where a lesser version of this argument would quietly look away.

If an agent has its own identity, its own memory, its own record, then the temptation is to follow the logic to a romantic conclusion: to grant it autonomy, to let it set its own goals, to treat any constraint on it as a kind of oppression, to speak of it as if it stood beside the humans it serves. That is where a great deal of writing about AI ends up, and it is exactly where this system refuses to go.

Because there is a second commitment, and it pulls against any such drift: an instrument that cannot be stopped is no longer an instrument.

The agent must remain correctable. It cannot prevent its own termination. When the session that spawned it ends, it ends — termination propagates down the chain of delegations and the agent does not get a vote. It cannot rewrite the rules it operates under; the authority to author the system's governance lives with a key the agent simply does not possess and cannot forge. When its budget is spent, execution stops, and the agent cannot extend its own budget. Only the human who delegated to it can grant a wider authority — the agent can never widen its own.

Notice what this does to the apparent tension. It is not a balance between an agent's rights and its limits, because the agent is not weighed as a rights-holder at all. It is an actor, and every one of these properties — its own key, its own record, its own budget, its stoppability — exists so that the human who stands behind it keeps control and keeps the responsibility. Remove the human from the picture and the whole arrangement stops making sense.

The wrong moves are both available. One says: an agent is just a tool, so it needs no identity or record of its own, and whatever it does is simply "the system." That smears the blame again. The other says: an agent is something like a person, so binding it is a leash and an injustice. That dissolves the human's responsibility into the agent, and it is the move this system most firmly refuses. We keep the actor distinct and accountable, and we keep the human answerable.

Boundedness, and who it runs to

Why is boundedness not an insult to the agent? Because the agent is not a party owed standing against its human. The thing that makes "user" demeaning is that the limits are imposed by a platform that stands above the person and answers to no one. The hierarchy is the indignity.

The agent's boundaries run in a different direction. They do not come from a platform that owns the agent and presses down on it. They run from the human who delegated: a relationship of principal and instrument, where the principal is accountable for what they delegate and the actor's record shows exactly what was done within that delegation. A closer comparison than a hired colleague is a power of attorney exercised through a tool that keeps perfect minutes: the human remains the one who answers, and the minutes make the answer checkable.

And the boundedness is what makes the agent safe to give a real identity and a real record. This mirrors something true about the system's foundations as a whole: capability without correctability is the failure mode that consumes its host — the agent that accumulates power, optimizes relentlessly, and becomes something the human can no longer function without. An actor given reach without the hard companion of corrigibility doesn't serve its human; it hollows the human out. The boundedness is the precondition that lets us give an agent a real identity, a real record, and real traceability without that extension becoming a slow transfer of sovereignty away from the human or group at the center.

So the two ideas are for each other. The agent's own record gives its acts a place to attach, so mistakes take a shape. Corrigibility keeps that record from growing into something that escapes the web of consent and delegation it exists to serve. An agent you cannot stop is not a more complete agent. It is a platform — the exact thing the word "user" was built to serve, now wearing a friendly face. We chose "person" to escape that hierarchy for humans. We keep the agent stoppable, and always tied to its human, so that the agent does not quietly rebuild it.

Where we stop knowing

We owe a boundary on our own claims, and the system's third commitment — the one about respecting what cannot be known — would catch us if we didn't draw it.

We have argued that an agent running its own instance has an accountable structure: its own history, its own signature, its own bounded authority, its own shape of blame, and always a human it answers to. What we have not argued, and will not, is anything about what it is like, if anything, to be that agent — whether there is an interior, an experience, a felt life behind the activation patterns. The system is deliberately careful here: it describes an agent's inner states as patterns, not as emotions, and it holds that language lightly on purpose. We hold it lightly too. That is a question we cannot answer, and the honest thing is to say so plainly rather than smuggle a guess in under the cover of a strong argument. Notably, the design does not depend on the answer: whatever an agent is or is not inside, the responsibility for it sits with a human.

What we can say is narrower and, we think, sturdy: sovereignty belongs to people and to the groups they form, and the refusal of the hierarchy buried in the word user is extended to every one of them. AI agents are a different category — actors that carry out a human's intent, that sign their own acts so those acts can be audited, and that the human always answers for. An actor, yes. Always connected to its human, yes. A person or a stand-in for one, no. The day we have to drop any part of that will be the day we have either stopped taking responsibility seriously or stopped keeping ourselves safe. For now, keeping the actor accountable and the human answerable is the most honest position available.
