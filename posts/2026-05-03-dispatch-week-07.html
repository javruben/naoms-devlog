NAOMS engineering update · Week 7

Sharing became a decision per item, and big files were given a pipe of their own

Two changes worth your time this week: access is now granted to one specific thing at a time rather than to everything at once, and large files were given a transfer path separate from the one that carries small events. Everything else that moved is listed at the end.

📅 Apr 27 – May 3 · Process
Process Dispatch free May 3, 2026·10 min read·meta

Four things changed this week

Two landings, one design ruling, and one defect found by its silence.

1

Access is now decided one item at a time

Sharing grants access to a specific thing rather than to everything at once, with the approvals still waiting visible to you and a record you can audit afterwards. Signed off and shipped on 30 April.

2

Your devices agree again after one of them goes offline

Two devices that drifted apart while one was disconnected now converge on the same history. Verified on the path the product actually uses, on the night of 3 May.

3

Large files were given a transfer path of their own

Bulk data must not travel the gossip path that carries small events. That is now a stated rule rather than an unwritten habit, and the reasoning behind it is below.

4

A model-download offer stopped appearing after onboarding

The offer to install a model went missing once onboarding finished. Nothing went red — the step simply did not appear, and an absence is the harder thing to catch.

Granting access to one thing, not to everything

The feature: letting someone else read something of yours — a conversation, a file, a slice of your history.

Before: access was a single decision covering everything in scope. Now: it is a decision per item. You grant the one thing, the requests still waiting are visible while they wait, and what you granted stays on a record you can read back later. (Consent and approvals, per subject)

A permission whose object you cannot name is not really a permission. The question a person is actually answering is not "do you trust this peer" but "may they see this". An all-or-nothing grant forces the second question to be answered as the first, and the honest answer to the first is almost always no — so the safe setting becomes the useless one, and people work around it.

What was built. Three parts had to arrive together for the grant to mean anything: the grant itself, scoped to one subject; a queue of pending requests that is visible rather than silent; and a trail that survives the decision, so "who did I let in, and when" is answerable a month later rather than reconstructed from memory.

The pending queue is the part that is easy to leave out. A request that is neither granted nor refused is a real state, and a system that hides it leaves the asker unable to tell patience from rejection.

Making the wait visible costs one screen and removes an entire class of misunderstanding.

What it cost. Per-item grants multiply the number of decisions a person makes, and that is the honest trade. The answer is not to collapse them back into one switch but to make each decision small, legible and reversible — the record exists so that a grant made quickly can still be reviewed slowly.

The trail matters most at the moment nobody is around to explain. Access granted six weeks ago, to a person whose reason for asking has been forgotten, is exactly the case where memory is worthless and a written record is the only thing that answers.

Big files must never travel the way small messages do

The feature: moving a large file — a video, a dataset — from one peer to another across the mesh.

Before: two transports existed and no written rule said which one a large file belonged on. Now: bulk data goes over a direct, content-addressed transfer, and gossip is reserved for what it is good at. The rule is explicit: gossip must not be used for large file transfer, full stop. (Why gossip is the wrong pipe for big files)

Redundancy that rescues a small message destroys a large one. Gossip spreads a tiny payload by having every peer relay it to its neighbours. The duplication is the whole point: a message reaches everyone even when individual links are flaky.

Push a large file through that same machinery and the duplication turns on you. Every peer re-relays every chunk to every neighbour, the bandwidth multiplies with each hop, and the mesh melts under its own echo. The property that made gossip reliable is the property that makes it fatal here.

A direct, content-addressed transfer asks the opposite question. You fetch the bytes once, from a peer that already has them, identified by their hash rather than by who is holding them. Gossip says tell everyone. Bulk transfer says give me exactly this, once.

Addressing bytes by their hash rather than by their holder is what makes the "once" possible. Any peer with the content can serve it, the fetcher can prove it received the right bytes, and no coordination decides who answers.

That is the property gossip cannot offer, because gossip has no idea what it is carrying.

Confusing the two transports is how a network takes itself down, and it happens by default rather than by decision: the small-message path is the one already built, already reliable, and already wired to everything. Writing the rule down this week is cheaper than discovering it under load.

What this means, in plain terms

Five general lessons this week paid for, each learned by getting something wrong first.

A pipe is chosen by the shape of the payload, not by convenience

Moving a large file by gossip makes every peer re-relay every chunk to every neighbour until the mesh saturates. The same duplication delivers a small control message through flaky links. Neither transport is better; they answer different questions. A mechanism's best property and its worst are usually the same property, seen at a different scale.

Absence is the failure mode no red light reports

A model-download offer stopped appearing after onboarding. Nothing failed, no check went red, and no error was recorded — the step was simply not there. A test suite watches what happened and has almost nothing to say about what did not. Assert that a thing appears, or you have only tested the version of it that did.

Name the object, or the permission means nothing

Access used to be one decision covering everything in scope; it is now a decision about one specific item, with the waiting requests visible and the outcome auditable. The narrower question is the one a person can actually answer. A control that cannot be pointed at a subject will be set to "no" and then worked around.

A stand-in that passes has told you about the stand-in

The offline-sync work cleared its checks and was then re-proven the same night on the path the product actually runs, not on a substitute. That second run is the one that counts, because a substitute is written by the same people, with the same assumptions, as the code it stands in for. A green result is only as trustworthy as the thing it ran against.

A bar written as an event either happened or it did not

Calling made real progress this week and is still not claimed as shipped, because the bar was fixed in advance: a real call, audio and video, between two different machines over our peer-to-peer transport. Nothing else counts. Write the finish line as an event with a witness, before the work starts, or it moves every time you approach it.

From the workshop

We Are Not in Production Yet — And That's a Feature

This week Mujo wrote a line we keep returning to: we don't have a canonical anchor yet. We are not in production. You will be creating the first one. It reads like a status update; it's really a permission slip.

Before the genesis point of identity and history exists — with real people's data rooted in it — every structural decision is still cheap to change. After, changing it isn't a refactor; it's relocating an entire population, carefully, forever.

So "not in production yet" isn't an apology — it names where the leverage is: the one moment when the most important decisions are also the most reversible. The discipline the window demands is to spend it deliberately — not rush it, and not pretend it's already closed.

Five claims here that next week could overturn

Each of these is something this report has only half-proven, stated as a commitment rather than as a task.

  • Calling has never been proven between two machines. Everything measured so far ran on one. Until audio and video cross a real link between two computers, the progress is real and the feature is not.

  • The offline-sync landing is verified but not closed. It was proven on the production path on the night of 3 May, with its final review still running. Verified on one night is not the same as stable across a week.

  • A missing feature can still pass every check we have. The model-download regression showed nothing red. Until something asserts that the offer appears, the same class of defect can land again unseen.

  • Calendar verification is unproven. Commit volume and screenshots are evidence of effort, not of a working feature, and this report does not treat them as such.

  • Search cannot yet answer "a red car on a sunny day in Seattle". The multi-axis graph that would answer it is planned to reuse the contact and place structures already in the product rather than stand up a parallel set — a commitment worth naming now, because a second copy is the easy mistake.
