One screen now holds every operator role, and the rules that govern NAOMS became readable data

Four things changed this week

One per major area, and the week's shape was front-loaded: busiest on 13 May, tapering toward the weekend.

1

Every operator role now lives on one screen

Finding a role, joining it and leaving it happen in a single place, replacing the per-feature screens they used to be scattered across. Checked against 181 screenshots before it was called done.

2

The rules that govern NAOMS became readable data

142 rules moved from hand-written evaluators to plain declarative phrases, and about 8,300 lines of the machinery that used to interpret them were deleted.

3

The daemon now uses your whole machine

The conversion of the native bridge from a blocking to a concurrent design continued through several waves this week. Its ceiling is now your hardware rather than a single thread.

4

Installing a model carries straight through to your devices

Choosing a model in the marketplace now reaches device assignment without a second step, and an invited member's devices bind end to end — landing as the week closed.

Finding a role should not require knowing where it lives

The feature: operator roles — the jobs a person or a machine can take on inside NAOMS — and the act of finding one, joining it, and leaving it again.

Before: each role was administered from the screen belonging to the feature that used it, so joining two roles meant knowing two places and learning two layouts. Now: one roster holds all of them, with the same three actions on every row. (One screen to find and enrol in every role)

A directory is worth more than the sum of the things it lists. Scattering role management across the features that use it is locally sensible every single time — the role belongs to the feature, so the screen does too. What it costs is the one question a person actually has, which is not "how do I join this role" but "what roles are there".

What was built. Beyond the roster itself, following a link straight into a specific role now lands you on that role inside the roster, rather than at the top of a list you then have to search. That is what makes the screen usable from outside itself: a message about a role can point at the role.

What it cost to prove. 181 screenshots dated 15 May stand behind this, covering the surface state by state rather than sampling it. (181 screenshots before we called it done) The last hard defect — an edge in steward authorisation, where the check for who may act on a role disagreed with itself — was closed on 17 May.

The same argument applies to the rules migration below, and to most of this week: the value of a change is rarely in the thing that changed, but in the number of places a person no longer has to look. Both landings this week reduce that number.

What is still open. The work is close to finished rather than finished. The roster is in and verified; the final review had not closed as the week ended, and this report says "nearly done" rather than rounding it up.

Rules you can read beat rules you have to run

The feature: the policies that govern how NAOMS behaves — what is allowed, what must happen first, what is refused.

Before: each rule was a piece of hand-written code, so the only way to know what a rule said was to read the program that enforced it. Now: 142 of them are plain declarative phrases — data the system reads — and about 8,300 lines of legacy interpretation machinery went away with them. (Deleting 8,300 lines: AGENTS.md becomes policy)

A rule expressed as code can only be audited by someone who can read code. That is the whole argument. A governing rule has an audience wider than the people who implement it, and every step that widens the audience without weakening the rule is worth taking.

Deleting 8,300 lines is the part that proves the migration was real. A conversion that adds a declarative layer on top of the evaluators it replaces has not replaced anything; it has added a second place for the truth to live, and the two will disagree within a month. The removal is the evidence.

The declarative form also changes who can object to a rule. A phrase can be read aloud in a discussion and disagreed with by someone who will never open the codebase, which is the whole point of writing rules down at all.

There is a cost, and it is worth naming. Declarative rules are only as expressive as the vocabulary they are written in, so a rule that the vocabulary cannot say has to either stretch the vocabulary or stay in code. The pressure that creates is real, and it points the right way — toward a small, stated set of things a rule is allowed to talk about.

What this means, in plain terms

Five general lessons this week's work paid for, each learned by getting something wrong somewhere first.

A directory answers a question the individual screens cannot

Operator roles were administered from whichever feature used them, which reads as tidy until someone asks what roles exist at all. One roster now answers that, and joining or leaving happens on the same row. When a thing is scattered by ownership, nobody owns the question "what is there?"

Deleting the old path is what proves the new one

142 rules became declarative phrases and roughly 8,300 lines of the machinery that used to interpret them were removed in the same landing. Had both stayed, there would now be two answers to every question about what a rule says. A migration that leaves the old mechanism running is an addition, not a migration.

Classify a write before it is stored, never after

A new piece of work routes every write through one classification first: a vow is a durable, governance-bearing commitment and is anchored; weather is ephemeral, high-volume state and is not. Intent should travel with the data, because a storage layer that has to infer intent from content will infer it differently next year.

An honest third answer beats a confident guess

That same classification has a third bucket — ambiguous — for writes that fit neither, and it surfaces them rather than picking one silently. A guess made at storage time is unrecoverable later, because nothing records that a guess occurred. Give every classifier a way to say "I do not know", or it will answer anyway.

Same-host green is a milestone, not a finish line

A three-peer call with audio, video and live transcription went green this week, all three peers on one machine. That is a real milestone and it is not the feature, which is a call between machines. Name the part of the path your test did not cross, in the same sentence as the result.

What would prove this week's biggest claims wrong

Four things this report has only half-proven, each stated as a commitment rather than as a task.

  • The three-peer call has never crossed a machine boundary. Audio, video and live transcription went green with all three peers on one host. Until frames cross a real link between two computers, this is a milestone and not a call.

  • The operator-roles screen is verified but not closed. 181 screenshots and a defect closed on 17 May are strong evidence; the final review was still outstanding when the week ended.

  • A declarative rule is only as good as the vocabulary it is written in. 142 rules now read as phrases. Nothing here shows what happens to a rule the vocabulary cannot express, and that rule is the one that will decide whether the migration holds.

  • "It got faster" is not yet routinely signed. The benchmark suite can produce signed datapoints. Until every such claim in these reports carries one, the claim rests on memory.
