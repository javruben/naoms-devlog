Week 19 · Signing into other apps without a key

Slack and Discord accounts connect by scanning a code instead of pasting one; a group's AI-admission history survives after the AI leaves; the build pipeline resolves its own merge conflicts; and recall learns to follow a chain of related memories.

Process Dispatch free August 8, 2026·12 min read·meta
TL;DR This week you can log Slack and Discord into your conversations by scanning a code instead of copying a key, a group's record of how an AI got admitted survives after the AI is gone, and the automated check that reviews changes before they ship now resolves its own conflicts instead of bouncing them back to a person. Four threads, one week — read on for all of them.

The week the account-linking screen stopped asking for a key, and three other changes rode alongside it.

Connecting an outside messaging account used to mean finding a settings page, copying a session key out of it, and pasting that key somewhere else — the kind of step that quietly filters out everyone except people willing to do it. This week that step became a scan of a code. Alongside it: a shared conversation's record of how an AI got admitted now survives after the AI is removed, the pipeline that checks a proposed change before it ships stopped bouncing every conflicting change back to a person, and the assistant's recall learned to follow a chain of related memories rather than only rank them by keyword.

The big picture

Connecting another account got a real login. Slack now connects by harvesting a workspace session straight from the browser — nothing to copy or paste — and Discord's in-app "Connect" now goes through the same browser-relay, scan-a-code path used elsewhere, which keeps working even where the direct route to Discord's servers is blocked. A direct 1:1 conversation with someone on a bridged account now lands in the ordinary Friends list instead of being filed as a group, and outgoing messages carry delivery receipts the way a native chat does.

The build pipeline stopped treating a disagreement as a rejection. When two proposed changes conflict, an automated reviewer now resolves the conflict itself, inside the pipeline, before evaluating the result — rather than bouncing the whole change back to a person to redo by hand. A change that gets updated after it already passed review is re-checked from the top instead of riding on the old verdict, and a change that fails and is retried re-joins the review queue at the back, not the front.

Recall learned to follow a chain, not just rank a list. The part of the assistant's memory search that can walk from one memory to a related one — rather than only scoring memories by keyword or similarity — is now wired into the path members actually use, rather than living only in test scaffolding. It ships off by default; turning it on is a separate, explicit choice. Search on a memory category with no configured limit now falls back to a sensible default instead of failing to answer.

A group conversation's AI-admission history became readable. Four moments — an AI proposed, an AI joined, someone objected, someone removed it — are shown in time order, in the conversation's settings, and the record keeps working once the agent it describes is gone.

Why it matters

A list of who is here cannot tell you how they got here. Membership is a fact about now. How someone arrived is a fact about the past, and the two need different storage — when the only record is the membership list, removing someone deletes the history of their arrival as a side effect, not because anyone decided it should be forgotten. (We wrote about that split earlier, in write to the log, read from the projection.)

An exact list beats a clever pattern wherever over-removal is the harm. The obvious way to filter a family of labels is a pattern that catches anything containing the word — which also catches "bald eagle" and "cosmetic dentistry." A plain list of whole labels, compared after collapsing spacing and case, removes what is on it and nothing else.

The point of a queue is that failing does not let you cut the line. A change that fails review and gets retried now re-enters the review queue where it currently stands, not where it stood before it failed — the same rule any fair queue for people applies, and the kind of detail that is easy to get backwards without anyone noticing for a long time.

Signing in without a key

Think of two phone carriers that have never agreed on a shared standard: to call from one to the other, you used to need the receiving carrier's own account credentials, copied out of its own settings screen and typed into the calling app by hand. Anyone unwilling to go find that key never made the call.

That was the shape of connecting Slack or Discord to a conversation here. This week both connections became a scan.

Slack. The new login harvests a live workspace session straight out of the browser the moment you're already signed in there — there is no key to locate, copy, or paste. The card presenting this connection was also narrowed to the one purpose it serves, replacing a more general approval surface that used to stand in for it.

Discord. The in-app "Connect" button used to route straight to Discord's own servers, which fails outright anywhere that route is blocked. It now goes through the same browser-relay, scan-a-code path already used for other connections — proof against exactly that kind of network interference, because the browser, not the app, is the thing doing the talking to Discord.

Where a message actually lands. A direct, one-to-one conversation with someone reached through a bridged account used to get filed the same way a group conversation is. It's derived now from the bridge's own membership data — is this conversation actually between two people, or more? — and a genuine 1:1 lands in the ordinary Friends list, the same place a 1:1 with anyone else lands.

What a sent message proves. Messages sent out through a bridge now carry the same delivery receipts a native conversation shows — sent, delivered, read — instead of leaving the sender to wonder whether anything arrived on the other side. And a location or a contact card can now be shared through a bridge the same structured way it can be shared natively, rather than only as a line of text describing it.

Creating a group. Starting a brand-new group conversation through a bridged account is now a real action inside the app — a popup that asks who belongs, and produces an actual room on the other side — rather than something you had to go create in the other app first and then discover here.

None of this changes what a bridge is: a translation between two systems that were never designed to talk to each other, so nothing here promises the two sides behave identically in every respect. What it changes is the part that used to filter out anyone unwilling to hunt down a key.

The sign-in book

A visitor's badge tells you who is allowed through a door right now. It is not the interesting artefact. The interesting artefact is the sign-in book, because it is the only thing that can still answer "who let this person in?" a month after they left.

Group conversations had the badge and not the book — not because nothing was written down, but because nothing read it back.

What is gathered. Four kinds of moment: an AI is proposed, an AI joins, someone objects, someone removes it. These were already being recorded as they happened; what is new is a panel that collects them and puts them in order. Note what is not in the list — an approval. The panel tells you an agent was proposed and that it subsequently joined; it does not show a separate entry for each member who said yes, even though those individual votes are themselves recorded elsewhere on the conversation's chain.

The request to admit one now has names on it. A request to admit an AI draws its own purpose-built card in place of the generic approval banner, carrying the agent's name, its role, and the name of the person proposing it — each shown when the inviter supplied it.

Why it outlives the agent. Removing an agent clears its participant record. If the account of how it got there lived in that same record, it would go too. Reading the events from where they were written instead means the history is still there when there is no agent left to point at — which is exactly the case in which someone is most likely to ask.

One conversation admits by a different rule than a shared space does. A group conversation that stands on its own, outside any larger shared space, admits an AI on its creator's say-so alone: the creator is its only administrator, and a proposer's own vote counts toward approval, so a creator proposing an agent also approves it, with nobody else acting. Anyone else's proposal there waits for the creator. A shared space with its own membership can instead record, per action, how much agreement that action needs — any member, administrators only, or a level no direct request can satisfy at all, refused outright with a message that the change needs a formal proposal. (On checking a caller at the gate rather than trusting it up front: trust the agent, verify at the gate.) Two things stay out of reach of that mechanism no matter how it's configured: appointing someone to a governance role, and handing out the powers that let someone set these rules in the first place. A space with no recorded rule for an action gets the strict, administrators- only behaviour it already had — and so does a space whose recorded rule turns out to be unreadable, which settles on the stricter answer rather than the more permissive one.

connecting an outside account stopped requiring a copied key, a group's AI-admission record became readable, a review pipeline started resolving its own conflicts, and the lines we've told our own checker to ignore went up again.

Four honest notes

  1. The skip markers moved the wrong way. 17,251 lines across the source tree carry a marker telling the automated checker to pass over them. Each is a place where a rule was set aside.
  2. The graph-walking recall stage ships off by default. It reached the real path members use this week, but turning it on is a separate, explicit step, and it is not yet the default.
  3. The panel does not show who agreed. Individual approvals are recorded on the conversation's own chain but are not among the four kinds of moment the history panel reads, so "everyone consented" is not a question you can put to it.
  4. The book library has shipped nothing for three consecutive weeks. Zero changed files in this window and in each of the two before it, while the messaging-bridge code alone took 107, 89 and 83 over those same three windows.
