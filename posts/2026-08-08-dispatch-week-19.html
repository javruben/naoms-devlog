Week 19: how the AI got in

The record of how an AI got into a group conversation — who proposed it, when it joined, who objected, who removed it — can now be read back, and it outlasts the agent.

Process Dispatch free August 8, 2026·9 min read·meta
TL;DR If someone brings an AI into your group chat, you can now read back how it got there — who proposed it, when it joined, who objected, who removed it — and that record stays readable after the agent is gone. Your shared spaces can also set how much agreement an action takes.

The first week in which you can ask a group conversation how its AI got there.

The events were already being written down. What was missing was any way to read them: the conversation could tell you who was in it right now, and nothing about how they arrived. Remove the agent and the list went empty.

This week the reading arrived — a panel that gathers those events and puts them in order, and which keeps working once the agent is gone. Around it, three smaller changes: what a request to admit an AI tells you, how much agreement an action in a shared space takes, and which labels a photo is allowed to show you.

The big picture

The record became readable. Four moments — an AI proposed, an AI joined, an objection, a removal — are shown in time order, in the conversation's settings.

The request to admit one has names on it. A request to admit an AI now draws its own purpose-built card in place of the generic approval banner, and that card carries the agent's name, its role and the name of the person proposing it — each shown when the inviter supplied it. The card is drawn only for this kind of request, so other approval screens are untouched.

A shared space can set how much agreement an action takes. In a shared space — a standing group with its own membership, distinct from the conversations inside it — installing an app can now carry its own rule, rather than one fixed rule for everything. The default is the rule that was already in force.

Two photo screens stopped showing labels about how you look. Machine-made labels describing grooming or appearance — wig, balding and the like — are dropped from a photo's own label list and from the labels shown beside a search result, using a plain list of whole labels rather than a pattern.

Why it matters

A list of who is here cannot tell you how they got here. Membership is a fact about now. How someone arrived is a fact about the past, and the two need different storage. When the only record is the membership list, removing someone deletes the history of their arrival as a side effect — not because anyone decided it should be forgotten. The fix was not to keep the departed agent listed; it was to stop asking the list and read the events instead. (We wrote about that split earlier, in write to the log, read from the projection.)

A new setting should default to the behaviour it replaces. Adding a control is a chance to change what happens to everyone who never touches it, and that is almost always a mistake. A space that has recorded no rule for an action gets the administrator-only behaviour it already had; a space whose recorded rule cannot be read gets the same, rather than the most permissive reading. The new control changes what you asked it to change and nothing else.

An exact match cannot over-match; a pattern can. The obvious way to filter a family of labels is a pattern catching anything containing the word, which also catches "bald eagle" and "cosmetic dentistry". Instead there is a plain list of whole labels, compared after collapsing spacing and case, so it removes what is on it and nothing else. Where over-removal is the harm, an auditable list beats a clever rule.

The sign-in book

A visitor's badge tells you who is allowed through which door right now. It is not the interesting artefact. The interesting artefact is the sign-in book, because it is the only thing that can still answer "who let this person in?" a month after they left.

Group conversations had the badge and not the book — not because nothing was written down, but because nothing read it back.

What is gathered. Four kinds of moment: an AI is proposed, an AI joins, someone objects, someone removes it. These were already being recorded as they happened; what is new is a panel that collects them and puts them in order. Note what is not in the list — an approval. The panel tells you an agent was proposed and that it subsequently joined; it does not show a separate entry for each member who said yes.

Why the four are scattered. They are not all written to the same place, so a reader asking one conversation for its history would see only part of the story. Instead the panel gathers all four kinds wherever they were written and matches each one to the conversation by the identifier the event itself carries.

What it will not do. It is a read-only view. Nothing in the panel creates state and nothing in it grants anyone anything — it cannot be the thing that admits an agent, only the thing that tells you one was admitted.

Why it outlives the agent. Removing an agent clears its participant record. If the account of how it got there lived in that record, it would go too. Reading the events instead means the history is still there when there is no agent left to point at — which is exactly the case in which someone is most likely to ask.

Where you find it. In the conversation's settings, on desktop and on phones.

Who may do what, per space

One rule per action, not one rule per space. A shared space can record, for a named action, how much agreement that action takes. The first action wired to it is installing an app; other actions can be wired to it. (On checking a caller at the gate rather than trusting it up front: trust the agent, verify at the gate.)

The two levels a direct request can satisfy. Any member — every active member passes. Administrators — an active member holding an administrator or steward role passes, a steward being a member trusted with the space's full governance authority. There is a third level no direct request can satisfy: an action set to it is refused outright, with a message that the change needs a governance proposal.

What cannot be reconfigured. Two things are excluded from this mechanism outright: appointing a steward, and handing someone a role that carries governance powers. A space cannot lower the bar on the actions that decide who sets the bars, and an attempt to record such a rule is refused when it is written, not when it is used.

The default, and the unreadable case. A space with no recorded rule for an action requires an administrator — the behaviour that was already in force. If a rule exists but cannot be read, the answer is also administrator: it settles on the stricter of the two levels a request can satisfy, not the looser one. The rules themselves are set by an administrator, not voted on by members.

In one line: the record of how an AI joined became readable, shared spaces gained a per-action say in who may act, and the lines we have told our own checker to ignore went up again.

Four honest notes.

  1. The skip markers moved the wrong way. 17,251 lines across 5,631 files carry a marker telling the automated checker to pass over them. Each is a place where a rule was set aside.
  2. The panel does not show who agreed. Individual approvals are not among the four kinds of moment it reads, so "everyone consented" is not a question you can put to it.
  3. A group conversation outside a shared space admits an agent on its creator's say-so. Its creator is its only administrator, and a proposer's own vote counts as an approval — so a creator proposing an agent also approves it, with nobody else acting. Anyone else's proposal waits for the creator.
  4. The book library has shipped nothing for three consecutive weeks. 48 files of code — a library, highlights, annotations, three catalogue sources — and zero changes in this window and in each of the two before it. Over those same three windows the conversation code took 89, 83 and 51.

The detail

Files changed between the two positions, counted under each area's own folder in the shared source tree. Each number is all of that area's traffic for the week, not only the change named beside it. These are the user-facing areas; most of the week's 1,243 changed files sit in plumbing not covered here.

  • Chat — 51. The record's read path and panel. The mobile mirrors are counted in the client trees, not here.
  • AI chat — 39. The named approval card.
  • Photos — 25. Appearance-class labels dropped from a photo's label list and from the labels beside a search result.
  • Shared spaces — 25. Per-action authority, and the approval flow around changing how a space is governed.
  • Calling — 11, vault — 10, voice — 7, canvas — 3. Maintenance.
  • Files browser — 1, calendar — 1. Quiet.
  • Book library — 0, notes — 0. Nothing this week.
  • Graph browser — 0 net. A merged-person badge landed and was pulled back inside the same window over a rendering fault. The net is zero; the week was not.

What's next

  • Bring the skip-marker count down; it is the one number here moving the wrong way.
  • Resolve names in the panel, so a row reads as a person rather than a shortened identifier.
  • Show a space's per-action rules where its members can see them, rather than only where they are enforced.
  • Read the individual approvals into the panel as well, so it can answer who agreed and not only that the agent joined.
