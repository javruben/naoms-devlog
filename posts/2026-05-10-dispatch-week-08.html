NAOMS engineering update · Week 8

Runaway test processes can no longer pile up and strangle the machine you are working on

One change worth your time this week, and it is not a feature: test processes that used to accumulate until they choked your machine are now recognised, supervised and kept away from your real data. Three separate causes had to close together for that to be true.

📅 May 4 – May 10 · Process
Process Dispatch free May 10, 2026·7 min read·meta

Four things changed this week

Three causes of one failure, closed in a single landing — and a week with no feature in it.

1

Orphaned test processes are now recognised as orphans

A stray test process used to look exactly like the real one to the cleanup that should have removed it, so it was passed over in silence and left running. The two are now told apart.

2

The watchdog has something watching the watchdog

The process that clears away runaway work had no supervisor. When it died, nothing restarted it and the protection simply stopped, without saying so. It is now restarted after a failure.

3

A test can no longer take over the live connection

A test process that fell back to the real data directory could claim the production socket and serve traffic meant for the real thing. That fallback is closed.

4

No feature shipped, and that is the honest headline

Some weeks you ship features. This week the whole of the work went into stability — small, hard, and real — and nothing else is claimed here.

When cleanup cannot tell a stray from the real thing

The feature: running NAOMS's own tests on your machine without them slowly taking it over.

Before: test processes accumulated. The cleanup that should have removed them classified them as the live process — they looked the same from the outside — and passed over them in silence. Now: an orphan is identified as an orphan and cleared.

A cleaner that cannot tell its target from its owner does nothing, safely. That is the worst shape a safety mechanism can take, because it fails in the direction that raises no alarm. Nothing crashed and no error was recorded; the count of processes just went up until the machine slowed to a stop.

The second half of the same problem ran in the opposite direction. A test process that could not find its own data directory fell back to the real one, and from there could claim the production socket — the connection the live system answers on. A test that reaches the real data directory is no longer a test.

Both faults come from the same missing distinction. Nothing in the system carried a reliable answer to "is this the real one?", so every component that needed one guessed. The two guessed in ways that happened to be exactly wrong: the cleanup treated a stray as real, and a stray treated the real data as its own.

A guard that dies quietly leaves nothing behind it

The feature: a watchdog that clears away work which has run away from its owner, so a stalled job does not sit on your machine forever.

Before: the watchdog had no supervisor. If it died, it stayed dead, and everything it was protecting against resumed unnoticed. Now: it is restarted when it fails.

Protection that ends silently is worse than no protection, because the system around it goes on being designed as though the guard were still there. Nobody adds a second defence next to a mechanism they believe is running. The absence is invisible precisely to the people who would fix it.

This is why the three causes had to close together rather than one at a time. Fixing the classifier alone leaves a cleanup that can be turned off by its own crash; fixing the supervisor alone leaves it faithfully restarting a process that cannot recognise what it is meant to remove.

Each fix is worth having only when the others are present.

What this means, in plain terms

Five general lessons this week paid for, each of them learned by getting the same failure wrong from a different direction.

A cleaner that cannot identify its target does nothing, safely

Stray test processes were classified as the live process, because from the outside they looked identical, so the cleanup passed over them. Nothing went red; the machine simply filled up. A safety mechanism that cannot tell its target from what it is protecting will always fail in the quiet direction, and quiet failure is the expensive kind.

Every guard needs a guard

The watchdog that removed runaway processes had nothing to restart it. Once it died, the protection ended and the system carried on as though it had not. A defence with no supervision has an availability, not a guarantee — and the difference only shows up on the day you needed the guarantee.

A fallback that points at production converts a test into an outage

A test process that could not locate its own data directory fell back to the real one, and could then claim the connection the live system answers on. Make the unsafe fallback an error. A default that reaches for real data is a convenience exactly until the moment it is a disaster.

One failure, three causes, one landing

The pile-up had three independent causes: a misidentifying classifier, an unsupervised watchdog, and a fallback into the real data directory. Any one left open reopens the whole symptom. Count the causes before you claim the fix — a partially closed failure behaves exactly like an open one.

Stability is a week's work with nothing to show at the end of it

Nothing shipped this week that anyone can point at on a screen. What changed is that a machine running NAOMS's own tests no longer gets slowly strangled by them. Work that removes a failure leaves no artefact, so it has to be reported deliberately or it reads as a week where nothing happened.

How much healthier is it than a week ago?

This week is counted in causes closed, not in volume. Three causes of one failure were closed together in a single landing, and no feature reached people.

Two honest notes

  1. The evidence for this fix is an absence. The symptom was processes accumulating until a machine slowed to a stop. What we can show is that they no longer accumulate — which is real, and is weaker evidence than a feature you can open and use.

  2. Nothing user-facing shipped. The one landing this week protects the machine you work on. That is the whole list, and it is stated as the whole list rather than padded.

In one line

stray test processes are now recognised, the watchdog that removes them is itself supervised, a test can no longer reach the real data directory or the live connection, and no feature shipped.

What would prove this week's stability claim wrong

Three things this report has only half-proven, each stated as a commitment rather than as a task.

  • A fix proven by absence is the weakest kind of proof. Processes no longer pile up on the machines we ran. Nothing here shows the classifier against a process shape we have not already met.

  • The supervisor has not been shown surviving its own bad day. It restarts the watchdog after a failure. What is not yet demonstrated is what happens when the supervisor is the thing that fails.

  • A week of stability leaves nothing a reader can open. The single landing here is invisible by design. Until the next window puts something on a screen, the honest description of this one is that it removed a way to lose your afternoon.
