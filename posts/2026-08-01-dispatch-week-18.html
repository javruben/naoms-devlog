Four things changed this week

One per area. The fourth added nothing to the product, and is the one the other three depend on.

1

Group members are pseudonymous by default

A shared group's member graph now forms from name-less, per-group tokens, so being in a group with someone no longer tells them who you are.

2

The group settings panel matches what you actually govern

Roles, permissions and credentials were one dense screen. They are now three things a person can name: Credentials, Trusted hives, Tokens.

3

Recall is now asking a real model, not a diagram

The layer that decides which of your memories is relevant to a question is tethered to a language model running in production, called on the live path.

4

A concluded speed-up was measured properly and un-built

Bundling the whole application to make loading faster was concluded a yes, re-examined, and closed: no, do not build. Nothing was built.

Belonging to a group without being named

The feature: joining a hive — a shared space where a group keeps a common canvas and conversation — without that membership handing your identity to everyone else in it.

Before: the member graph was keyed on names. Two people who both knew the same friend produced a single node with that friend's name written on it, and auto-sharing a non-member into a group could ship their name, email, phone number and even their root identity in the clear.

Now: the member graph forms from name-less, per-hive contact commitments — a per-hive-salted token rather than a name — so two members who know the same person still converge on one node, and that node carries no name at all.

The convergence is the part that had to survive. A group needs to know that two members are talking about the same person, or it cannot merge anything. The old design bought that by using the name as the key, which quietly made the name readable by everyone who could see the graph.

A join key is a disclosure. Whatever value two parties must agree on in order to recognise a shared record is, by construction, visible to both of them. Choosing a person's name for that role hands out the name; choosing a salted token per group hands out nothing that travels between groups.

How your name gets out, when you want it to

Your name is revealed only when you deliberately disclose it, per hive, through a real selective-disclosure credential. The signatures underneath are BBS+ over a pairing-friendly curve, which lets you prove one claim about yourself without handing over the whole document that contains it.

Auto-sharing is hard-clamped to the pseudonymous level, on both the shared rows and the consent receipt. That is a clamp rather than a default, which matters: the automatic path cannot be talked into the generous behaviour, and the generous behaviour requires a person to act.

And when your own device already knows who a pseudonymous node is, it labels it for you locally, with no network call at all. Strangers stay strangers, friends look like friends, and nobody else learns which is which.

This one was completed on 2026-07-27 rather than left in progress. It is the clearest expression this stretch of a rule we keep returning to: belonging to a group should not cost you your name.

The speed-up that measured well and helped no one

The feature: none. This is a piece of work that was concluded, re-examined, and closed as do not build.

Before: the week prior, bundling the whole frontend and backend into one or a few files had been concluded a yes. Measured carefully, the win held up: boot and load got dramatically faster for users far from the server, on high-latency links.

Now: it is closed. No, do not bundle, anywhere. Nothing was built.

The question that dissolved it was four words long: what far users? The entire frontend loads from local disk. The desktop shell loads from its own bundled daemon on the local machine. The phone loads its assets in-process. The daemon refuses to bind to the open network at all without an explicit override.

There is no far user. The 7.6-second load that made bundling look like a win was latency the measurer had injected into the test themselves. The numbers were sound and the world they described does not exist.

Why this is in a report about what shipped

We are telling you about a feature that is not there because the discipline that killed it is the same one that makes everything else here worth reading. A speed-up you cannot feel is not a speed-up.

A benchmark whose conditions never occur is a story rather than a measurement. Running the numbers and then throwing them out is not a side-quest; it is the part of the process that gives the other claims in this report their weight.

What this means, in plain terms

Five lessons this week paid for. Each came from getting something wrong first, and each survives outside this codebase.

A benchmark's conditions are part of its result

Bundling looked like a large win against a 7.6-second load. That load came from latency injected into the test by the person running it, and no user on any supported path experiences it. The measurement was correct and described nowhere. State a benchmark's conditions beside its number, then check that they occur.

A join key is a disclosure

Two members had to agree on some value to recognise that they knew the same person. Using the name made the graph merge correctly and made every name readable. A per-group salted token merges just as well and travels nowhere. Whatever two parties must share to agree is a thing you have published.

Clamp the automatic path; make the generous one deliberate

Auto-sharing is hard-clamped to the pseudonymous level, on the shared rows and on the consent receipt both. Disclosure requires a person to act, per group. An automatic path should not be able to reach the permissive setting at all, rather than merely default away from it.

Resolve privately what only you need to know

When your device already knows who a pseudonymous node is, it labels it for you locally, with no network call. You see a friend's name; the group sees a token; nobody learns that the two are connected. A lookup that never leaves the device leaks nothing, which is a stronger claim than a lookup that is trusted.

A label that implies structure the system does not enforce is a lie

The old settings panel framed the space around "curators and roles," implying a hierarchy the system does not actually impose. The redesign drops the framing and names the three things a person governs. Interface vocabulary is a claim about the model underneath; keep it accurate or change the model.

Three honest notes

  1. The settings redesign is not finished. It has landed and is still being refined. We will keep saying so until it is done rather than describing an in-progress surface as a milestone.

What changed, area by area

Every area that moved this window. Per-area file counts are not available for this window under a rule we can reproduce, so these are named without a numeral rather than given an invented one.

Shared groups.

A hive's member graph now forms from name-less, per-hive contact commitments — a salted token rather than a name — so two members who know the same person converge on one node carrying no name.

Auto-sharing is hard-clamped to that pseudonymous level, on the shared rows and the consent receipt, closing a case where it could ship a non-member's name, email, phone or root identity in the clear. Your name is revealed only by deliberate per-hive disclosure, through a credential built on BBS+ signatures over a pairing-friendly curve. Your own device labels a node it already recognises, locally, with no network call.

Group settings.

The configuration surface was rebuilt around one question: what does a person actually govern here?

The old panel mixed roles, permissions and credentials into a single dense screen; the redesign splits them into Credentials, Trusted hives and Tokens, lets self-registered plugins surface their own panes, and drops the "curators and roles" framing that implied a hierarchy the system does not impose. It has landed and is still being refined.

Memory and recall.

The retrieval layer — the part that decides which of your memories is relevant to a question — is now tethered to a real language model running in production, with the routing to call it wired into boot.

It is the seam between search that finds text and a system that reasons over what it found, and it is now a live path rather than a diagram. Early, not finished.

Loading and bundling.

A concluded optimization was re-measured and closed without being built.

Bundling the whole frontend and backend into one or a few files was a real win against a 7.6-second load — latency the measurer had injected into the test — and every supported path loads locally: the frontend from local disk, the desktop shell from its own bundled daemon, the phone in-process, with the daemon refusing to bind to the open network at all without an explicit override. No far user exists, so nothing was built.

Chat.

Groundwork landed for protocol parity across bridged conversations, so a conversation carried in from another network can behave the same way a native one does.

Vault.

A run of fixes landed on the unlock path taken during boot.

What the refusal bought, and the four places this week is unproven

Each of these is real and narrower than it will sound repeated back. Each names the gap rather than the task.

  • Pseudonymity holds between groups, not inside one. Per-hive salted tokens mean nothing travels across groups. Whether someone already in a group can infer who you are from what you say, and when, is a different question this week does not answer.
  • The refusal rests on today's topology. No far user exists: every supported path loads locally, and the daemon will not bind to the open network without an override. If that stops being true the answer changes, and nothing records what would trigger a re-examination.
  • Recall calls a model; it has not been shown to be better for it. The tether is live on the real path. Nothing here measures whether the answers improved, which is the only reason to have built it.
  • A redesigned panel has not been used by a stranger. Credentials, Trusted hives and Tokens are the three things we think a person governs. Nobody who did not build it has yet been asked whether those are the three things they went looking for.
From the workshop

The Refusal Is the Feature

It would have been easy to keep the bundling win. It measured well, the code worked, and nobody outside would ever have known the lab conditions were invented. Killing it cost a week's conclusion and bought nothing you can put on a changelog.

That's exactly why it matters: a project whose whole premise is honesty doesn't get to be honest only when it's cheap. That is the system declining to tell a convenient story, and it's the through-line of Week 18 — under a real privacy promise, the same refusal to lie.
