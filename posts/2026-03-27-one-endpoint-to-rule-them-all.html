One Endpoint to Rule Them All

Collapsing four separate network doors into one authenticated transport on Iroh

Technology Architect paid · premium preview March 27, 2026·6 min read·blob-swarm
TL;DR Every message between devices now comes through one authenticated door instead of four, and the app that used to occasionally hang on startup now starts in under a second. Here's how we found the duplicate doors, merged them, and the one test we shipped still failing — on the record.

Most refactors that matter don't add a capability. They remove a coincidence — the accident of four things having grown up separately when they were always the same thing. This is the story of one of those, and it landed in a single day.

The home page of iroh, the peer-to-peer networking project whose single authenticated endpoint we adopted as the one transport underneath everything.

On 2026-03-27, NAOMS went from four duplicate Iroh endpoints to one. The work carries the slightly comic name "Iroh Startup Hang → Unified Transport," because it began as a bug — the daemon would sometimes hang on startup with Iroh enabled — and ended as an architecture. The design audit that day put the real problem in writing: four duplicate Iroh endpoints, all unified into a single transport bridge.

Four endpoints. Each one opening its own QUIC listener, each one with its own notion of who was allowed to talk to it, each one a separate place a startup could wedge. The fix was not to make four endpoints faster. It was to notice they were one.

flowchart LR
  P[peer message] --> B[one transport bridge]
  B --> A[authenticated as a DIDComm envelope]
  A --> D[your daemon]

Why four endpoints happened

It's worth being honest about how a system ends up with four of something it needs one of, because the answer is never "someone was careless." It's that each endpoint was reasonable when it was written.

NAOMS grew its peer-to-peer surface in layers. Hives needed LAN transport first — an early milestone proved a three-machine LAN with checkpoint anti-entropy. Then hives needed to reach the wider internet — the WAN story added gossip, batching, lazy sync, S3 backup, compaction. Calls and chat signaling needed a path. The MCP daemon needed a path. Each of those arrived with its own Iroh endpoint because, at the moment it was built, that was the smallest change that worked.

The trouble with "smallest change that works" applied four times is that the fifth time you go to reason about the system, there is no single answer to "where does a message enter?" There are four. And a startup sequence that has to bring up four listeners has four ways to block — which is exactly the hang that opened the whole effort. A non-blocking-accept fix — so the server starts cleanly with Iroh enabled — treated the symptom. The duplication was the disease.

The unification: one transport bridge, every message a DIDComm message

The design landed in three steps over the course of the 27th, and the shape of the decision shows in the work itself: replace the four separate listeners with a single transport bridge, route every peer-to-peer message through DIDComm with an auth bootstrap, then audit the result to confirm all four duplicate endpoints were gone.

Two ideas are doing the work here, and they reinforce each other.

The first is one endpoint. Instead of four QUIC listeners, there is a single transport bridge. Everything that used to open its own Iroh endpoint now routes through it. Startup brings up one listener, not four; there is one place a message can arrive, one place to reason about backpressure, one place a hang could happen — and that one place was made non-blocking.

The second, and the more important one for the security story, is every message is a DIDComm message — all peer-to-peer traffic flows via DIDComm over the single Iroh endpoint.

This is the part worth slowing down on. Before unification, "is this peer allowed to send me this?" had up to four different answers depending on which endpoint the bytes hit. After unification, there is one answer, and it's enforced at the door: a message that arrives on the transport bridge is a DIDComm message or it is nothing. DIDComm gives you sender authentication and encryption as a property of the envelope itself — the transport doesn't carry anonymous bytes that some later layer has to decide whether to trust. The guiding question the effort set for itself names the whole ambition in one line:

"What if every P2P message was authenticated, every transport was unified, and NAOMS started reliably every time?"

Three wishes, one endpoint. Authentication stops being a check you remember to do and becomes a thing the transport simply is.

The wiring: what actually changed under the hood

Concretely, the duplicate endpoints were collapsed by giving each former caller a handle to the shared bridge instead of its own listener. The Android client, which had been speaking its own dialect, was migrated in the same arc — its WebSocket client and call signaling moved onto the bridge too.

That migration matters because it proves the unification wasn't just a server-side tidy-up. Call signaling — the most latency-sensitive, most fiddly P2P path in the system — moved onto the unified transport too. If the bridge could carry call setup, it could carry anything. The end-to-end test added the same day exercised exactly this: full browser login, chat, call, and Iroh transport verification in one run.

There's a smaller, quietly excellent result buried in the startup work. An earlier end-to-end test in the arc asserts a hard number: the server starts with Iroh enabled in under a second, 8 of 8 cases passing.

Sub-second startup with the full P2P stack enabled is the opposite of where this began (a hang). The same audit that removed the duplication removed the thing that made startup unreliable, because they were the same bug seen from two angles: four listeners is both "harder to reason about" and "four chances to wedge."

The honest part: 31 of 32

Here is where we want to resist the tidy version of this story, because the closing record is more honest than a celebration usually is. The unified transport shipped — single Iroh endpoint, all DIDComm — at 31 of 32 end-to-end cases passing.

Thirty-one of thirty-two. Not thirty-two. The work shipped — marked done and verified — with one end-to-end case still red, and the closing note says so rather than rounding up. We don't know, from the record alone, which case was the holdout or why it was judged acceptable to ship with it failing.

A caveat, unverified: we have not traced the specific failing 32nd case or the rationale for shipping at 31/32; we're reporting the count exactly as the record states it, and flagging that the one red case is real and unexplained here, not a rounding artifact.

That single failing test is, to us, the most trustworthy thing in the whole arc. A "unify everything in one day" refactor that reported a clean sweep would be the thing to distrust. One stubborn red case, named in the celebrate commit, is what a real cutover looks like.

Why it's built this way

Step back and the principle is the same one that runs through the rest of NAOMS: make the safe thing structural, not optional. Four endpoints meant authentication was a policy each endpoint had to remember. One endpoint, where every message is a DIDComm envelope, means authentication is a property of the only door there is. You can't forget to authenticate a message that the transport refuses to carry unauthenticated.

The duplication this removed wasn't waste in the "extra lines of code" sense. It was waste in the dangerous sense: four answers to a security question that should only ever have one. Collapsing them didn't just delete code. It deleted ambiguity — and on the path where peers reach into your daemon from across the internet, ambiguity is the whole attack surface.

What's next is the WAN-scale work that rides on top of this foundation — the gossip, lazy-sync, and blob-swarm machinery the hive transport work began — now that every one of those messages travels the same authenticated road. The hard part was never making transport fast. It was making there be only one of it.
