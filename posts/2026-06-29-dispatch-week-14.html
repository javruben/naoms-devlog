NAOMS engineering update · Week 14

NAOMS stopped being a thing you run in a shell and became a thing you open

Four separate pieces of work this week agreed on the same sentence: NAOMS is something you open rather than something you start. None of them is finished, and none of them opened a path out to a server. Everything else that moved is listed at the end.

Four things changed this week

One per area, and each one is a way in rather than a feature inside. None of them is finished, and the substrate underneath all four is the same governed, local-first daemon it was last week.

1

NAOMS became a window you double-click

The whole memory system, daemon included, now runs inside a single native Mac application. The browser tab and the terminal startup sequence are gone, and the bundle is signed and notarized so macOS vouches for it on sight.

2

The real daemon now runs on an Android phone

Not a remote control pointed at a server: the governed runtime itself runs on the device, in full production governance mode, with the graph pipeline behind it and your memory unlocked by an actual fingerprint rather than a stub.

3

It can speak, and the voice never leaves your machine

Speech is generated by a text-to-speech model running on your own machine and kept warm, so it answers without a cold-start stall. No hosted speech service is called, and no sentence of yours leaves the device to be read aloud.

4

Two people held a conversation through it

Two human beings talked end to end, on their own devices, with nothing in the middle reading along. It is the first such conversation rather than a finished messaging product, and the wider chat work remains in implementation.

A Mac that trusts the app before it opens

The feature: installing and opening NAOMS the way you open anything else on a Mac — double-click, and it is running.

Before: open a terminal, start a daemon, point a browser at a local port, and hold the whole arrangement in your head while you use it.

Now: one signed, notarized application carries the daemon inside it, and macOS vouches for it on sight rather than warning you about it.

Building the window was the visible half. The half nobody celebrates is the signature: a Developer-ID signature and Apple notarization on both the disk image and the application itself, so the operating system has already checked the bundle before you double-click it.

Trust has to arrive before the first run, or it does not arrive. The "unidentified developer" warning is not a small blemish on an otherwise good first impression. It is the first sentence the system says about you, and it says it while the person still has the option of not continuing.

The signature also carries a second guarantee that has nothing to do with appearances: it proves the bits were not altered between our machine and yours. That is the part that makes the absence of a warning honest rather than merely convenient.

We also made the bundle self-contained, so it carries its own native dependencies and runs wherever it lands rather than depending on what happens to be installed. One command produces the signed, notarized, self-contained result.

The scope, stated plainly

This is macOS first, and only macOS. The desktop work names all three desktop systems; we drove the Mac path first because notarization is the hardest of the three to get right.

Linux and Windows are the road ahead, not this week's claim. Getting the hardest one honestly done teaches the other two; it does not ship them.

The daemon actually runs on the phone

The feature: NAOMS on an Android phone — your memory, your keys, your governed runtime, on the device in your hand.

Before: a phone could only ever have been a thin window onto a daemon running somewhere else, which is a different product wearing the same name.

Now: the governed daemon itself runs on the phone, in full governance mode, with the graph pipeline behind it and your memory unlocked by a real biometric.

The word real is carrying weight in two places there. The daemon is the actual one, not a reduced phone edition. The unlock is an actual fingerprint check, not a stubbed approval that always returns yes.

Build first the part you cannot retrofit. If the governed daemon does not run on the device, nothing built on top of it is local-first in any honest sense — and no amount of later work can move that floor underneath a surface already standing on it.

What this is not yet

This is a foundation, not a finished phone. What landed is the governed daemon running on the device in production governance mode, the biometric unlock, and the graph pipeline behind them — the three things that had to be real before anything above them could be.

The phone experience people will actually use is still being built on top of that floor. We are marking the floor because it is the part that had to be true first, not because it is the part you can see.

What this means, in plain terms

Four doorways opened this week — a window, a phone, a voice and a conversation — and the pattern underneath them is the same one in every case. It is worth stating separately, because none of the four articles above would say it alone.

The surface may get friendlier; the substrate may not get weaker

Four new ways in landed this week — a window, a phone, a voice, a conversation — and not one opened a path out to a server. At every one of those forks, leaning on a hosted service would have shipped sooner. A friendlier front door is only a gift if it did not quietly install a back one.

A hosted convenience puts a hole in the promise it is convenient about

The voice could have been a hosted text-to-speech call, shipped a week earlier and sounding just as good. It is local instead, because the moment your words leave the device in order to become sound, "stays on your machine" has an exception in it. A promise with one exception is a default, not a promise.

Changes counted are churn, not distance travelled

Week 13 logged 5,586 commits. This week logged 1,648 and is by a distance the most user-visible week the project has had. Signing an application or wiring a fingerprint moves few lines and moves a person's hand a long way in. The commit graph is an honest measure of churn and a poor proxy for reach.

Trust is spent before the first run, not after it

A Developer-ID signature and notarization mean macOS has already vouched for the bundle when the person double-clicks. Without them the system's first statement about the product is a warning, delivered while not continuing is still easy. Whatever greets a person before they can act is your real first sentence.

The narrow true thing beats the wide promised one

Two people held one real conversation, peer to peer, with no intermediary. The promised version — a thousand conversations across every messenger, bridged and unified — is not built, and saying so costs the headline nothing it was entitled to. A first instance is evidence; a vision is not, and the two should never share a sentence.

Three honest notes

  1. The desktop app is macOS only. Linux and Windows bundles do not exist. The Mac path went first because notarization is the hardest of the three.

  2. The Android build is a floor, not a phone experience. The governed daemon runs on the device with a real biometric unlock and the graph pipeline behind it. The surface a person would actually use is still being built on top of that floor.

  3. Chat is one conversation, not a messaging product. The bridged, multi-network version is unbuilt, and this week proves only that the peer-to-peer path carries a real conversation between two people.

What changed, area by area

Every area that moved this window. Per-area file counts are not available for this window under a rule we can reproduce, so these are named without a numeral rather than given an invented one.

The desktop application.

The whole memory system, daemon included, now runs inside a single native Mac window, so the browser tab and the terminal startup are gone.

The same interface you reached through a local port now lives inside the application; a native shell supervises the daemon underneath it. One command assembles the application itself. ( NAOMS is now an app you double-click .)

Signing and distribution.

The disk image and the application both carry a Developer-ID signature and Apple notarization, so macOS vouches for the bundle on sight instead of warning about an unidentified developer, and the signature proves the bits were not altered in transit.

The bundle is self-contained: it carries its own native dependencies and runs wherever it lands. One command produces the signed, notarized, self-contained result. ( A Mac that trusts the app before it opens .)

The phone.

The governed daemon itself now runs on an Android phone, in production governance mode, with the graph pipeline behind it and memory unlocked by a genuine fingerprint rather than a stubbed approval.

This is the part that cannot be bolted on afterwards: without the governed daemon on the device, nothing above it is local-first. What landed is that floor, not the full phone experience. ( Your whole memory system in your pocket .)

Voice.

Four claims here that rest on a single instance

Each of these is real and each is narrower than it will sound when repeated back by someone who read only the headline. Each names the gap that remains rather than the task that would close it.

  • One conversation is not a messaging product. The peer-to-peer path carried a real talk between two people once. Nothing here shows it holding a hundred conversations, or any conversation that crosses into another network.
  • The phone has a floor and no rooms. The governed daemon runs on the device with a real biometric unlock. What a person can actually do on the phone above that floor is unbuilt, so "NAOMS runs on Android" claims less than it sounds.
  • One platform out of three is not a desktop story. Only macOS has a signed, notarized, double-click application. Until Linux and Windows have one, the desktop claim is a Mac claim.
  • A warm local voice has not met a slow machine. The model is kept warm so it answers without a cold-start stall, on the machines we ran it on. Whether that holds on hardware weaker than ours is untested.
