Dispatch · Week 14: The Week It Left the Terminal

A quiet-by-the-numbers week that moved a loud line. NAOMS stopped being a thing you run in a shell and started being a thing you open, hold, and hear — while two unglamorous repairs kept the workshop behind it from buckling.

Process Dispatch free June 29, 2026·9 min read·meta
TL;DR Week 14 was the week NAOMS stopped being a thing you run. A double-click Mac app, a notarization macOS trusts on sight, the real governed daemon running on an Android phone, a local voice, the first true two-person conversation — five steps that all point one way: out of the terminal and into a body. Told straight, shipped vs partial, each with a source — including the honest reason the most user-visible week of the project was also one of its lowest-commit weeks.

For most of its life NAOMS has been a thing you run. You opened a terminal, you started a daemon, you pointed a browser at it, and the system existed in the space between those commands. This week, five separate pieces of work quietly agreed on a different sentence: NAOMS is a thing you open. A window you double-click. A phone you unlock with your thumb. A voice in the room. A message that reaches another human being. None of these is finished. All of them are real. And the throughline underneath them is the one that matters most: the surface got friendlier without the substrate getting weaker. The same governed, local-first daemon is underneath every new doorway. We just built the doorways.

Five new ways in; one unchanged substrate underneath; and — the line the whole week turns on — not one of the five opened a path out to a server.

By the numbers

  • 1,648 commits in the seven-day window 06-22 → 06-28 (552 merge, ~1,096 non-merge). Treat that cautiously, as always: this era runs roughly a hundred concurrent agent worktrees, each landing through its own merge commit, so the merge count is fleet shape, not feature count.
  • Busiest day: 2026-06-23 — 378 commits (the day the desktop app and its dist mac assembly were landing together), tapering through the week (06-24: 311, 06-27: 249, 06-28: 100).
  • Here is the honest, interesting number: Week 13 logged 5,586 commits. This week logged under a third of that — and it is, by a distance, the most user-visible week the project has had. That inversion is not a slowdown. It is the signature of the work changing kind. Re-encryption backends and async refactors move thousands of lines; signing a .app, wiring a fingerprint unlock, or keeping a text-to-speech model warm moves comparatively few. The commit graph measures churn. It does not measure how far a person's hand now reaches into the system. This week those two diverged hard.
  • Net LOC: deliberately not quoted. At this volume and with packaging artifacts in the mix, a line count is dominated by bundled dependencies and generated files. We report landings, not lines.

Out of the terminal: the desktop

NAOMS is now a double-click Mac app. The whole memory system — daemon and all — is wrapped in a single native window (a Tauri v2 shell that supervises the daemon underneath), so the browser tab and the startup ritual are gone. The same UI you ran behind a localhost port now lives inside the application. That landed on origin/main this week as the desktop-app shell plus a naoms dist mac --shell=tauri command that assembles the .app itself.

Building the app was only half of it. The other half is the part nobody celebrates and everybody depends on: a Mac that trusts the app before it opens. A Developer-ID signature and Apple notarization on the .dmg and .app mean macOS vouches for the bundle on sight — no scary "unidentified developer" warning, and a guarantee the bits weren't tampered with on the way to you. We also made the bundle self-contained, so it carries its own native dependencies and runs wherever it lands. One command, naoms dist mac --notarize, produces the signed, notarized, self-contained result.

Honest scope: this is macOS-first. The desktop-app star names all three desktops; we drove the Mac path first because notarization is the hardest of the three to get right, and getting the hardest one honestly done teaches the rest. Linux and Windows are the road ahead, not this week's claim.

Out of the terminal: the phone

The same line moved on a second front. NAOMS now runs as a real Android app — and the word real is load-bearing. This is not a thin remote into a server somewhere. The actual governed daemon (the on-device runtime) runs on the phone, in production governance mode, with your memory unlocked by a genuine biometric — your fingerprint — not a stubbed-out approval. That is exactly the part you cannot fake later and bolt on afterward: if the governed daemon doesn't run on the device, nothing built on top of it is local-first in any honest sense. The foundation is what landed.

Honest scope: foundation, not polish. What shipped is the daemon running on the device with real biometric unlock and the graph pipeline behind it. The full phone experience is still being built on top of that floor.

Out of the terminal: a voice, kept home

NAOMS can speak to you out loud now, in a warm, natural voice — and the whole point of how we did it is where the voice comes from. The speech is generated by a text-to-speech model running locally on your own machine, kept warm so it answers without a cold-start stall. No cloud text-to-speech service. No sentence of yours shipped off to a server to be read aloud and logged on the way. This one landed and was celebrated this week (close-gate signed), which is why it sits in "shipped genuinely" below rather than "partial."

It is worth pausing on why that matters past the novelty. It would have been far easier to call a hosted voice API and ship a nicer demo a week sooner. We didn't, because the moment your words leave the device to become sound, the local-first promise has a hole in it that no UI can paper over. Even the voice stays home. That is the same instinct as the on-device Android daemon and the serverless chat below: the doorways got friendlier, and not one of them opened a back door out to a server.

Out of the terminal: two people talked

The quietest line of the week carried the most under it. Two human beings held a real conversation through NAOMS chat — end to end, on their own devices, with no server in the middle reading along. The chat surface was driven through its acceptance walk on origin/main (a real-daemon browser run, backed by a long defect register the work burned down), and on the far side of that walk, two people actually talked.

Honest scope, stated plainly: this is the first heartbeat, not the finished organ. The chat epic remains in implementation. The headline vision — a being holding a thousand conversations across every messenger, bridged WhatsApp and Signal and Telegram all unified in their own hands — is still being built. What's true today is narrower and still worth marking: the first real two-person conversation went through, on the peer-to-peer path, with no intermediary. A vision article makes a promise. This is the first time the promise made a sound.

Underneath: keeping the workshop standing

None of the five doorways above gets built without a place to build it, and two of this week's least glamorous landings are the reason the rest could happen.

First, we moved the fleet of AI sessions onto Zellij — a terminal workspace — so that a hundred concurrent agents behave like an organized board of panes you can list, attach to, migrate, and recover headlessly, instead of a tangle of windows that falls over under load. When the thing building your product is itself a swarm, the swarm needs a floor.

Second, we repaired the governance socket. The standard interface our agents use to reach NAOMS's rules and memory had drifted into a broken state: a capability lookup that failed on tool calls, and a login that popped a dialog on every reconnect. We fixed the seams — the lookup serves cleanly again and the grant no longer interrupts a reconnecting session. This one is honest about its own genre: it is a repair, logged against a post-celebrate bug register, not a new capability. Sometimes the week's most load-bearing work is making something that already shipped stop quietly failing.

The honest ledger

Told straight, by status, each line sourced above:

  • Shipped (genuinely): the warm local voice (celebrated, close-gate signed); the Mac app as a double-click window; the Developer-ID-signed, notarized, self-contained macOS bundle behind one command; the Android on-device governed daemon with real biometric unlock; the Zellij desk workshop and the MCP socket repair.
  • Partial — real, not finished: chat is one true two-person conversation into a long multi-messenger road; the desktop app is macOS-first of three; the Android build is foundation, not the full phone experience.
  • Not claimed: Linux/Windows desktop bundles; the bridged-messenger unification; anything beyond the macOS notarization being externally audited.

We mark these on purpose. Celebrate the partial wins; never imply they're finished. A doorway you can walk through is a real doorway even when the rooms behind it are still being framed.

What's next

  • Carry the desktop app to Linux and Windows — the same one-click, the same self-contained bundle, on the two platforms the Mac path went first to learn.
  • Build the phone experience on the on-device floor — now that the governed daemon and biometric unlock run on the device, the surface above them.
  • Turn the first conversation into the many — the multi-source chat unification the first heartbeat was the first beat of.
From the workshop

The Cheapest Week Was the Loudest

The number that should stay with you from this dispatch is the divergence: a third of last week's commits, and several times the user-visible reach. Distribution and embodiment are low-churn, high-consequence work. Signing an app, warming a voice model, wiring a fingerprint to a vault — these are small diffs that move a person's hand a long way into the system. It is a useful correction to the instinct that progress equals volume. The commit graph is an honest measure of churn and a poor proxy for how much closer the thing got to the people it's for. This week those two numbers pointed in opposite directions, and the smaller one was the truer one.

From the workshop

Every New Door, the Same Lock

Five new ways in this week — a window, a phone, a voice, a chat, an app icon — and a pattern worth naming under all of them. Not one opened a path out to a server. The Android daemon runs on the device. The voice is synthesized on the machine. The conversation went peer to peer. The Mac bundle carries its own dependencies and asks no remote service for permission to exist. It would have been easier, at every one of those five forks, to lean on a cloud and ship sooner. The discipline that says the surface may get friendlier but the substrate may not get weaker is the whole reason any of this is worth using. A friendlier front door is only a gift if it didn't quietly install a back one.
