Every Messenger in One List, and None of Them in Disguise

The unified inbox we're building pulls in conversations bridged from other apps — Telegram, Signal, WhatsApp and more. For a while those threads opened empty: the code that gathered a conversation's history knew how to read a native NAOMS thread but skipped bridged ones entirely. This week we taught it to read them too — and made a rule that every message arrives wearing its true origin, read straight from the record, never guessed.

Technology Teacher free July 2, 2026·4 min read·chat
TL;DR NAOMS is learning to hold every messenger you use in one calm list — your native NAOMS conversations alongside threads bridged in from Telegram, Signal, WhatsApp and others. Until this week, opening one of those bridged threads showed nothing: the messages had arrived and been stored, but the part of the app that gathers a conversation's history simply stepped over bridged channels. We fixed that — and made a rule of it. Every bridged message now renders with its true origin attached, read straight from the message's own record, never invented. You see "via Telegram" because that is where it came from, not because the app made a guess.

A unified inbox is only as trustworthy as its labels.

The Chat surface we're building has an ambition that sounds simple and isn't: put every conversation you have — the ones native to NAOMS and the ones that arrive from other apps you've bridged in, like Telegram or Signal — into a single calm list, collapsed by person, ordered the way you prefer. One room for everyone you talk to.

This week a piece of that landed, and it's worth telling honestly, because it started as a bug and ended as a principle.

The conversations that were there all along

If you bridged in a messenger and opened one of those threads, you saw an empty room. Not an error — just nothing. The unsettling part: the messages had arrived. They'd been received, and stored, and were sitting in the graph the whole time. The app simply wasn't looking at them.

Here's why. When you open a conversation, the code that assembles its history reads from a couple of places. One reads the native, direct thread between two people. Another sweeps the channel for its decisions, polls and shared packs — and that sweep, quite deliberately, stepped over any channel that came from a bridge. Between the two of them, nobody ever read the plain messages of a bridged channel. So a Telegram thread had a home, and a name in your list, and not a word inside it.

Teaching it to read the bridged room

The fix adds a third place to look. When you open a thread, the app now asks a quick question first: is this a bridged channel? It can tell two ways — from a mark on the row itself, or from the channel's own name, which for a bridged thread starts with where it came from (a Telegram thread's identity literally begins with telegram). If the answer is yes, it goes and reads that channel's stored messages directly and lays them into the same list as everything else.

flowchart TD
  A[You open a conversation] --> B{Is this a bridged channel?}
  B -- No --> C[Read the native NAOMS thread]
  B -- Yes --> D[Read that channel's stored messages directly]
  D --> E[Attach each message's true origin, taken from the message itself]
  C --> F[One list, in order]
  E --> F
  F --> G[Every bubble shows where it came from — via Telegram — never guessed]

That's the whole shape of it. Two rooms of logic became three, and the empty bridged thread filled in.

The part that's a principle, not a patch

The interesting decision isn't that we read bridged messages — it's how we label them. Every message that comes in from a bridge renders with a small mark of its origin: via Telegram, via Signal. And that label is not decorative, and it is not inferred. It is taken, verbatim, from the message's own record. If the message says it came via Telegram, that's what you see. If a message somehow carried no origin at all, the app would not invent one to make the room look tidy.

This is a small instance of something we hold to everywhere in NAOMS: the system does not quietly dress one thing up as another. A bridged message is shown as a bridged message. It doesn't get to masquerade as native just because it now lives in your NAOMS list. You are never left guessing whether the person on the other end is reachable through NAOMS itself or through a bridge to some other network — because the message tells you, and we pass that through untouched.

It would have been easy to skip. Once the messages render, a casual eye wouldn't miss a provenance badge. But an inbox that unifies everything has exactly one way to betray you: by blurring where things came from until you can't tell a private, end-to-end NAOMS thread from a message that travelled across someone else's servers first. The label is how the calm list stays honest.

What's true today, and what isn't yet

Plainly: this is a piece, not the finished thing. The unified Chat surface is still being built — bridged threads reading their own messages, with honest provenance, is one stage of a longer walk — proven so far by a focused test that runs the real message-reading code against a stand-in message store, not yet shipped for you to lean on end-to-end. We'll say "you can now" when it's earned. Today the honest claim is narrower and, we think, still worth it: the bridged conversations that were invisible are visible, and each one arrives wearing its own name.

That's the one calm list we keep describing, taking one more real step toward existing. It builds on the work of bringing a messenger in in the first place, and it's cut from the same cloth as the checks a message passes before NAOMS will believe where it came from.
