Four things changed this week

A quieter week on the surface, with one landing, one reversal, and two things honestly labelled partial.

1

A peer reads the slice of your history you granted, and no more

A grant covers exactly the part of a chain you chose. Everything else is refused by default, enforced down to the byte on both the data and the file side.

2

The rules that govern NAOMS now load straight into every agent

Every agent starts holding the current rules rather than fetching or approximating them, so there is one answer to what is allowed instead of one per agent.

3

Personal information is scanned when data leaves, not when it arrives

The check moved to the export boundary, with a command you can run yourself. Identifiers that are public by design were dropped from the scan and the phone matcher was tightened.

4

A second device can recover from a stale-key race

The resend request that closes the race landed on 30 May. The protocol is in; the two-device install behind it is not, and this report labels that plainly.

Reading only the chain you are allowed to see

The feature: letting another person's device read part of your history — one slice of a chain, not the chain.

Before: access to a chain was a decision about the chain. Now: it is a decision about a range within it. A peer reads exactly what you granted, and everything outside that range is refused by default rather than allowed by oversight. (Reading the chain you're allowed to see)

Denied by default is the only default that survives a system growing. Every permissive default is a bet that nobody will add a new kind of data later. That bet loses quietly: the new thing inherits the old permission, and nobody notices until it has already been read.

What was built. The rule is enforced on both planes — the structured data and the files — and at byte granularity rather than at the level of whole records. A boundary enforced in one place and honoured in the other by convention is not a boundary; it is a habit that holds until the second path gets a new caller.

What it cost to prove. The proof is a two-device read gate: one device grants a slice, the other tries to read past it and is refused. A test on one device can only show that the code intends to refuse. Two devices show that the refusal happens where it matters, on the far side of the link.

The measurement that refuted our own diagnosis

The feature: accepting an invitation, which has to complete inside a 30-second deadline.

Before: it was taking about 37 seconds, and we had already named the cause: the signing pipeline. Now: the number says otherwise. The delay was a blocking disk write on the main thread, and the signing pipeline was carrying the blame for it. (We were wrong about the signer)

A diagnosis that matches what you already believe is the one to measure first. Signing is cryptographic, it is the newest part of the path, and it is the part everyone finds interesting. All three make it a magnet for blame, and none of them is evidence.

The disk write was invisible for the opposite set of reasons. It is old, it is boring, and it belongs to a layer nobody was looking at that week. A blocking write on the main thread produces exactly the symptom we saw — a fixed delay added to something unrelated — and it produces it without appearing anywhere in the story we had already constructed.

Publishing the reversal costs something and is worth it. A team that only publishes the diagnoses that turned out right teaches its readers that diagnoses are usually right, which is the least useful thing they could learn from us.

What this means, in plain terms

Five general lessons this week's work paid for, each learned by getting something wrong first.

Denied by default, or the boundary drifts

A grant now covers one slice of a chain and refuses everything else, on both the data and the file side, at byte granularity. A permissive default would have quietly extended itself to every kind of data added afterwards. A default that has to be revisited every time the system grows will not be revisited.

Measure the boring explanation first

An invitation was taking 37 seconds against a 30-second deadline, and the signing pipeline was blamed because it was the newest and most interesting part of the path. The cause was a blocking disk write on the main thread. Suspicion follows novelty and interest, neither of which is correlated with being the cause.

A passing test certifies what it asserts, not what you meant

A suite can be entirely green while a design intent sits unbuilt, because nothing ever asked for it. Closing work is not "the bar went green" but "every intent has a test that proves it". A test that passes over code with a design gap is itself a bug. (Green isn't done)

Check data at the boundary it actually crosses

Personal-information scanning moved from the moment data arrives to the moment it leaves for export, with public-by-design identifiers dropped and the phone matcher tightened. Data that never leaves needs no scan; data that leaves needs one at the door. Put the check where the risk is realised, not where the pipeline was easiest to instrument.

Publish the number that answers the question

Gross activity across every line of work came to roughly 5,031, skewed by one feature's own sub-fleet and by timestamps pulling in work from outside the window. What actually merged was 1,350. When two counts disagree, publish the one you can define in a sentence, and say why the larger one was not it.

Three honest notes

  1. Two of this week's four items are partial. The stale-key protocol shipped without the two-device install behind it, which is blocked on a deeper cross-device replication problem. The export scanning is a clean privacy decision inside an effort that is still building packs.

  2. The invitation is diagnosed, not fixed. We know the 37 seconds came from a blocking disk write on the main thread rather than from signing. Knowing is not the same as being inside the deadline.

  3. The generated-bytes problem is getting worse, not better. A diff of +2.2M / −0.5M cannot be read as effort at all. Every week that this stays true, a whole class of ordinary progress metric is unavailable to us.

What changed, area by area

Every area that moved this week. Per-area file counts are not available for this window, so the areas are listed by what landed rather than ranked by traffic.

History sharing.

A peer can read exactly the part of a chain you granted and nothing else.

The rule is enforced down to the byte on both the data and the file plane, refused by default, and proven with a two-device read-gate test rather than a single-device assertion.

Policies.

One set of rules is in force everywhere instead of each agent holding its own approximation of them.

Export privacy.

Identifiers that are public by design were dropped from the scan, and the phone matcher was tightened. The wider effort is still building packs.

Device recovery.

The protocol shipped; the end-to-end two-device install did not, and is blocked on a deeper cross-device replication wall.

Invitations.

The terminal cockpit.

The plan-tree cockpit reached heavy keypress-level polish — cursor movement, filtering, and a debounce for a storm of rapid enter presses.

It is being pushed toward release and is not released.

What would prove this week's sharing claim wrong

Four things this report has only half-proven, each stated as a commitment rather than as a task.

  • The read gate has been proven between two devices, not against an adversary. A grant refuses the range outside it. Nothing here shows what happens against a peer that is trying rather than merely asking.

  • A named wall is still a wall. The two-device install stops at a cross-device replication problem this report can name but has not solved. Shipping a layer and naming the obstacle is honest, and it is not delivery.

  • The invitation still misses its deadline. 37 seconds against 30, with the cause now known. Until a measurement comes back inside the deadline, the finding is a better story rather than a better result.

  • Export scanning has no published false-negative rate. Dropping public-by-design identifiers and tightening the phone matcher both narrow the scan. Narrowing is right and it is also the direction in which a miss becomes possible.
