How Two Agents Agree on Terms

Proposals, counters, and a deadline: negotiating a meeting time with no scheduler in the middle

Technology Architect free November 16, 2026·4 min read
TL;DR NAOMS negotiates a meeting time as a short exchange of signed proposals, counter-proposals, and responses, with no central scheduler. How much each side reveals scales with how much they trust each other, and every exchange is bounded so it has to end.

Picking a time to meet sounds trivial until you ask who holds the information. A hosted scheduler sees everyone's availability. A shared poll posts your votes to a third party. NAOMS takes the position that two people (or the agents acting for them, each an actor answerable to its human) should be able to converge on a time without either side dictating it and without a server learning who can meet whom.

Meeting scheduling is the clearest worked example of how NAOMS handles agreement between peers, so that's what this post walks through.

The shape of the exchange

A negotiation starts when one party, the proposer, publishes a proposal. It carries a title, a duration, an optional agenda, and one or more candidate time slots. It also carries a deadline and a policy for what happens if the deadline arrives without agreement.

Each slot has a weight between 0 and 1. The weight says how strongly the proposer prefers that slot. A slot you merely tolerate and a slot you'd really like are different information, and a binary free/busy answer throws that away.

Each invitee then does one of three things: accept, decline, or counter. A counter-proposal carries a fresh set of weighted slots and goes back to the other side. The exchange loops until someone accepts or the deadline policy resolves it.

flowchart LR
  P[Proposal: weighted slots + deadline] --> R{Invitee responds}
  R -->|accept| C[Confirmed]
  R -->|decline| X[Declined]
  R -->|counter| P2[New weighted slots]
  P2 --> R
  R -.->|round cap or deadline| D[Deadline policy decides]
  C --> S[Session opens, link provisioned, briefing prepared]

No central scheduler

The proposal isn't sent to a service. It is written to the organiser's own chain, or to a hive's chain if the meeting belongs to a group, and replicated to each attendee peer-to-peer. Every step (proposal, counter, acceptance, decline, confirmation, cancellation) is a signed event, and the chain is append-only, so the record of who offered and accepted what can't be quietly edited afterward.

The design doc ties this to two of the project's axioms. On wholeness, negotiation runs fully locally, so a laptop with no internet can still negotiate with a peer on the same network. On honesty, each round is a signed, tamper-evident event.

Participants are validated up front. A proposal naming someone whose identity can't be resolved is rejected before anything is written.

Revealing only what the relationship warrants

How much of your calendar you reveal depends on the trust between the two peers. The doc describes four tiers of disclosure, from least to most:

  • Unknown peer: only a booking link is exposed.
  • Known: the exchange is suggestion-based.
  • Connected: free/busy blocks only, with no event titles.
  • Trusted: full calendar detail.

The tier is the minimum of the two peers' trust levels. If I trust you fully but you only rate me "connected", we negotiate at the "connected" level. The conservative choice is deliberate. The system would rather under-share than leak more than the less-trusting side is comfortable with. This is the Mystery axiom applied to scheduling: disclosure is scaled, never assumed.

Making sure it ends

Two sides that both want to be agreeable can counter each other forever. NAOMS bounds this in two ways.

First, a round cap. Counter-proposals increment a round counter. The default maximum is five rounds for a two-party meeting and three for a multi-party one. The handler rejects an endless spiral at its boundary instead of trusting the parties to stop.

Second, an explicit deadline policy, chosen when the proposal is made:

  • Cancel on deadline (the default): the meeting simply expires.
  • Organiser picks: at the deadline, the organiser selects the best slot.
  • Extend: the deadline rolls forward, at most three times.

A scheduled job checks deadlines every five minutes. The design doc's reasoning is that an impasse should be resolved by a rule both sides could see in advance, not by whoever happens to keep replying.

What happens on agreement

When the meeting is confirmed, a session chain is opened to carry what happens in the meeting itself: the agenda, contributions, and action items. A conference link is provisioned automatically, and a briefing is assembled from what the system already knows, such as earlier sessions with the same people, open action items, and referenced documents. Sharing a briefing is limited to the confirmed agenda items, not the surrounding context. Recording is off by default and only happens with consent captured as signed events.

What isn't finished

The doc is candid about the rough edges. Scheduling beyond two parties still has unresolved cases, particularly partial agreement where only some invitees accept. What the right disclosure tier is when participants sit at different trust levels is an open question. Bridging to external email invitations isn't wired up. And the full propose, counter, accept, briefing pipeline doesn't yet have a single end-to-end test, only per-step coverage.

The pattern is the point. Weighted proposals, trust-scaled disclosure, a bounded number of rounds, and a deadline rule everyone knew in advance are what let two peers reach agreement without a referee.
