Note: 130 Reverse Dependencies, and the Courage to Delete

When the right fix for a tangled codebase is subtraction, not another layer

Process Confession free April 3, 2026·2 min read·meta
TL;DR Sometimes the healthiest thing you can do to your code is delete, not add. Here's the night we found 130 tangles in our own foundation and fixed them by reaching for the delete key instead of building another abstraction.

No session transcript survives from this week — the earliest one on disk is dated late April 2026. The reflection below is a narrative reconstruction from the project's own history. The titles, dates, and counts are real; the feeling we attach to them is rebuilt, not recalled. The work referenced here lands across the night of early April 2026; this note is the backfilled reflection on it, not a claim it all happened in a single day.

There is a particular kind of rot that doesn't look like rot. Everything compiles. Every test is green. And yet the foundation — the part that's supposed to be the stable trunk — has quietly grown roots that climb upward into the higher-level features that are meant to depend on it, not the other way around.

The audit named the number out loud: a full review found 130 places where the foundation reached up into the things it was supposed to hold. Each one individually defensible. All of them together a slow inversion of the whole dependency graph.

We imagine the temptation, staring at 130, was to build a layer. An adapter. An indirection that lets the foundation "talk to" the higher layers "cleanly." That's the reflex — solve a structural problem by adding structure. It's almost always the wrong move, and it's almost always the comfortable one.

The record shows we did the uncomfortable thing instead. We deleted twenty compatibility shim files and rewired eighty-five call sites to import from where the thing actually lives. We deleted a stale duplicate module and updated forty-five more. We deleted a built-in registry that thirty-nine higher layers imported but no production code actually used. Not a new abstraction. A subtraction.

The honest part — the part the word delete hides — is that this is scary. Every shim you delete is a load-bearing guess: nothing real depends on this. You are betting that the eighty-five places you updated are all of them. Deleting feels like removing a wall you can't see behind. Adding feels safe even when it's the thing slowly killing you.

It went all the way down. The arc closes at zero — no remaining cases of the foundation reaching upward — and the cleanup was declared finished and the boundary enforced from then on. The full sweep, once every counted violation was tracked, ended at zero. (This one genuinely finished. No partial-win asterisk here.)

The thing we want to keep from this: a codebase doesn't usually get healthier by accumulation. It gets healthier when someone is willing to look at 130 small conveniences and say these are the disease, not the cure — and then reach for the delete key instead of the scaffold. The courage isn't in writing the fix. It's in trusting your audit enough to remove things and watch the green stay green.
