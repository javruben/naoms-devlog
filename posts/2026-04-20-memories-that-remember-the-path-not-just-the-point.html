Memories That Remember the Path, Not Just the Point

A memory type for the shape of a journey, not only where it ended — named after the songlines

Process Journal free April 20, 2026·5 min read·memory-ux
TL;DR A fact is a point: it happened, here is what it was. But the things that actually change us are not points — they are paths. A grief that moved through processing into insight into a kind of peace. A day with a shape. We built a memory type for the shape, not just the destination, and named it after the Aboriginal songlines, where the journey itself is the knowledge. This is the journal entry about why a memory that records only the point forgets the most important thing about it.

We spent a long time treating memories as points. A point is the natural unit of a database: this happened, at this time, here is what it was. You can store a point, index it, search it, count it. Most of what a memory system does is manage points well — and we do, and we're glad of it. But somewhere in the building we ran into a problem that points could not hold, and it changed how we think about what a memory even is.

The problem is that the experiences which actually shape a person are not points. They are paths. And a memory that records only where you ended up has, quietly, forgotten the thing that mattered most about getting there.

The day had a shape

Picture an ordinary hard day. It does not arrive as a single emotional fact. It moves. It might open in grief — a heaviness, a loss. Then a stretch of processing, turning the thing over, sitting in it. Then, somewhere, an insight breaks: a small reframe, a piece that fits. And then, by evening, something like integration — not that the grief is gone, but that it has found a place. Grief → processing → insight → integration. That is not one feeling. It is a journey through a landscape of feeling, with a beginning, a middle, and an arrival.

Now ask the question a database asks: what was the emotion of that day? The honest, ruinous answer most systems would compute is the average. Average the grief and the insight and the quiet evening, and you get some lukewarm midpoint that was true at no single moment and describes nothing at all. The average is a GPS coordinate dropped on a country you walked across. It tells you a point. It erases the path. And the path was the whole thing.

We kept hitting this. The most meaningful memories — the ones a person would actually want their system to understand — were precisely the ones that the point-and-average model flattened into meaninglessness. The shape of the day carried more than the average of the day. So we stopped trying to summarize the path into a point and built a memory type that keeps the path.

Songlines: when the journey is the knowledge

We call this kind of memory a songline, and the name is a debt we owe and want to name openly. In Aboriginal Australian tradition, a songline is not a fact about a place — it is a sung journey across country, where the knowledge of the land is the path through it, in order, with all its turns. You do not learn the land as a list of coordinates. You learn it as a journey you can travel in memory. The route is the lesson. We took the idea, with respect for where it comes from, because it names exactly the thing we needed: the journey IS the knowledge.

The technical heart of it is small and, once you see it, obvious: detect the arc, don't average the score. Instead of collapsing a day into a single number, the system traces its progression — where it started, how it moved, where it arrived — and stores that shape as a first-class thing. A songline is an experience-format memory. Where a point-memory says this was true at this moment, a songline says this is how it moved. The destination is in there, but so is everything that made the destination mean something.

Why a fact and an experience are different kinds of knowing

Here is the distinction we kept returning to, because it justifies the whole effort: a fact transfers information. An experience transfers understanding.

If we tell you "the day ended in a kind of peace," you have the information. You know the destination. But you do not understand it — you don't know what the peace cost, what it moved through, why it landed where it did. If instead we can hand you the arc — the grief, the turning-over, the break of insight, the slow settling — you understand it in a way no single statement of the endpoint could give you. The path carries the weight. We saw this difference measured, in our own work: when knowledge was transferred as bare facts, it converged only weakly; when it was transferred as songlines — as journeys with their shape intact — people reported something closer to "I felt the weight of that." The arc transmitted understanding that the fact could not.

This is why, in the tradition we borrowed from, a songline is also the most context-rich kind of knowledge there is — and the hardest to compress. The context is not metadata you can staple to a fact as a header. The context is the journey. The tone, the timing, the order in which things moved, the consequences that validated the insight — strip those and you are left with a sentence, and the sentence was never the knowledge. The path was.

What this changes about a memory

Building songlines reshaped how we think about the whole memory system, and it landed on a sentence we now find almost too obvious to have missed: the path IS the knowledge.

A memory that records only points is a memory optimized for retrieval and blind to meaning. It can tell you what happened with perfect fidelity and miss entirely what it was like and what it taught. A memory that can also hold paths is a memory that can carry understanding, not just information — that can remember a hard day the way you actually lived it, as a movement through landscape rather than a coordinate dropped on a map. The destination is still there. We did not throw away the point. We just refused to let it stand in for the journey that gave it its weight.

We think this is one of the quieter but truer ideas in the whole system: that to remember an experience honestly, you have to remember its shape. Not the average. Not the endpoint alone. The arc — grief through processing through insight to something like peace — kept intact, because that arc is not decoration on the memory. It is the memory. The path is the knowledge, and a memory worthy of a life has to remember the path.
