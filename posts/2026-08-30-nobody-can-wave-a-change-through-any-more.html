A pipeline with no approval step

Changes reach the product through a 28-stage pipeline with no approval step in it — a reviewer can refuse a change, but nothing and nobody can pass one. The hand-driven merge lane stopped on 23 August, and a cutover is the day you switch the old thing off and find out what it had been quietly absorbing.

Process Dispatch free August 30, 2026·10 min read·how-we-build
TL;DR If you use this project, the changes that reach you now go through a 28-stage pipeline with no approval step in it — a reviewer can refuse a change, but nothing and nobody can pass one. Here is how that path works, and what turned out to be broken once the old hand-driven lane switched off and it had to carry the load alone.

How a change gets into the product

A change reaches the product through a pipeline: a fixed sequence of steps a proposed change must pass, in order, before it becomes part of the product. The sequence is written down in a single file — just under 3,000 lines, describing 21 pipelines, of which the one guarding the main line (the single shared version of the project that releases are cut from) runs 28 stages.

A stage is one step in that sequence. Some of them judge the change — three reviewers read it, the types are checked, the whole test suite runs. Others do work: pulling in the latest main line, resolving conflicts, building a working copy, merging, publishing documentation and downloads. Both kinds count toward the 28, and it is worth being exact about that, because "28 checks" would be the wrong denominator; the judging set is roughly half.

That dashed box is the argument. A conventional pipeline has an approval step there — a named person or team who signs off, after which the change is cleared to land. This one does not. The instruction, in the plain-language equivalent of the words used, is: no change request must be approved by any human or machine, and the pipeline description must say specifically that no approval step is necessary, because the pipeline itself is the approval step.

Two things about that are easy to misread.

Judging is not approving. Three of those 28 stages judge the change, and they are not mechanical checks at all — they are language models reading the actual difference a change makes, one looking at design, one at tests, one at code. Any of them can reject it. What none of them can do is pass it. There is no verdict any reviewer can render that means "cleared to land"; they can only fail to find a reason to stop it. Rejection and approval point in opposite directions, and only one of them is delegated here. (A previous verdict is never a floor, either: a reviewer running again renders a fresh judgement and is free to reject what was let through before.)

A refusal cannot be re-rolled. Three of the reviewers are language models, so the obvious worry is that a rejected change could simply be resubmitted until the dice land green. It cannot: submitting the same branch at the same commit is treated as the same request and starts no new run. A fresh verdict requires a fresh commit — which is to say, actually changing the code. Nothing in the path lets a human overrule a refusal either.

The default is the opposite of this, and the fallback is loud. The software running this pipeline ships with the human approval gate on — a pipeline description that says nothing about approval gets one, which is the secure default and the right one for most people. This project turns it off deliberately, on one line. And if an approval stage is present anyway, the runner steps over it and announces that it did, emitting a recorded event, rather than either forging a signature or parking the change forever on one nobody is coming to give. Anyone running the same software with no configuration of their own gets an approval step, not this.

The older, hand-driven route now refuses on sight, naming what happened to the last people who used it: more than twenty submissions had been enqueued into a queue nothing was draining, and they simply sat there. Past that refusal there is a single documented escape, and it will not run unless you type a written reason that then gets recorded. How often it has been used, we do not know — the commit history is the wrong place to look for it, and we have not looked in the right one. Worth saying anyway, because a bypass you do not mention is just a default you have hidden.

The final stages are the ones that put documentation and downloads in front of people, over a network that needs no server.

What was wrong

A cutover looks finished long before it works. The demonstration goes fine. Then the new thing carries real traffic for the first time and reports, for hours, that it has completed zero of its stages — while the small server it is quietly hammering reboots twice under the load.

That happened here on 26 August, three days after the old route was switched off.

What actually changed, and when

The interesting thing about this cutover is that the pipeline was not new. It had been running for weeks: its own merges appear in the main line on 22 of the 25 days from 5 to 29 August. Three days are missing, and two of them are 22 and 23 August — the pipeline goes quiet on exactly the days the handover happens.

What ended on 23 August was the other lane — one automated lander that a person started, watched, and restarted whenever it wedged. Measured rather than assumed, that lander puts changes in every day through 22 August, 169 of them on the 18th and 174 on the 20th. On 23 August it lands 5. It never lands anything again.

So this was not the day the new thing switched on. It was the day the old thing switched off, and the pipeline had to carry the load alone. What follows is what that exposed.

The part nobody puts in the diagram

It was copying the whole project and throwing the copy away. Before its first stage, every run made a full copy of the repository across the network from a small machine — 2 cores, under 4 GB of memory, no swap, holding an 18 GB object store — and did this up to 10 times at once. Measured during the outage: load 24.9, 80 MB of memory left, 7 packing processes each holding 420–485 MB. Every run reported the same thing for hours: zero stages executed. The machine rebooted twice. And the copy had been pointless since 6 August, when a later stage started building the working copy properly and replacing the copy with a link to it — so for twenty days the pipeline paid 2.4 GB of network transfer per run for something it discarded on arrival.

A disk check measuring the wrong disk. The test machine's scratch area was a 31 GB in-memory filesystem, completely full, holding 29,073 abandoned temporary directories — the same class of self-inflicted mess as in evicting our own evidence. Everything sent there died within seconds. The health probe called the machine ready the whole time, because it was measuring the disk the project lived on, which was 32% used and perfectly fine. A true answer to the wrong question. Two real changes sat 49 hours with no verdict. The same fix caught a subtler sibling: the "is this disk full" test required a machine to be both above 97% and below 10 GB free, which let a small disk at 90% with 2 GB left sail straight through.

A connection that could never come back. A listing command had been running 7 hours at 0% processor. Its connection was still marked established; on the far end there was no connection and no process at all. That machine had rebooted 3 minutes and 37 seconds after the command started — and a reboot sends nothing, no close, no reset. The waiting side sits there forever. Keepalives — the periodic "are you still there?" probe that would have noticed — were off by default, and no connection timeout was set, so nothing was ever going to rescue it. This ran every 5 minutes, which means an outage on the far side stranded a fresh immortal pair every five minutes until somebody noticed.

A watchdog killing workers that were still working. Of the last 10 processes it killed for being idle, all 10 had been producing real output and died over a single gap between two pieces of it. One had produced 167 pieces of output first. The count of work already done was collected, displayed — and never once consulted by the rule that decided to kill. The fix was not a longer timeout but a different question: allow a gap up to three times the largest gap this particular run has already survived. A slower machine or a harder problem moves that window itself, with nobody retuning anything. (Measuring processor time instead was weighed and rejected: a process waiting on a model's response is idle by construction.)

Two scheduled jobs that had never once succeeded — not lately, ever — and, more to the point, the silence that let that stay true. A third could never have run at all.

And running through all of it, one defect wearing many faces: infrastructure failures were being recorded as verdicts on the change. A machine running out of space is not evidence that your work is wrong. Neither is a network fault, a snapshot failure, or a batch killed by a signal — a killed batch is a run that never got judged, which is a different thing from a run that was judged and failed. Each one had to be taught to say "the machine broke" instead of "you broke it."

Two paragraphs we had to take back

Two claims made during that fortnight were withdrawn, both on the same day, both against the project's own findings. The first had asserted that a whole class of scheduled work had never run. The reason recorded for the error:

"I relayed the broad version without a source because it was convenient to the conclusion I was already arguing."

The second withdrew a measurement taken from a command that had already failed — the number it printed looked like an answer, and nobody checked whether the command had exited cleanly. That retraction then went after its own shape:

"I named the right failure mode when retracting, then re-committed it under that name, so the caveat read as diligence."

Naming a bias is not escaping it. A caveat written well enough gets read as care by everyone, including the person who wrote it. It is the same trap as the bug report that was wrong, which was the finding — and this article walked into it three times while being written. A draft said the old lander was the only thing landing changes across eight days; two other identities appear in that window. A draft said everything now goes through the pipeline; the record shows the team also landing by hand that same week. A draft said the pipeline's merges appear every single day from 5 August; they appear on 22 of 25 days. Every one of those counts was correct. Every universal built on top of one was wrong — which is exactly the shape of the first retraction above, and the reason it is quoted rather than paraphrased.

Where this honestly stands

Working. The hand-driven lane is gone and refuses new submissions. The pipeline is genuinely carrying work, and has been for most of the month. Every defect in the repair list above is fixed and landed.

It is only fair to give the new lane's numbers, having given the old one's. Daily arrivals on the main line since the handover run 9, 8, 2, 12 and 9 — against 169 and 174 on the old lane's busiest days. Some of that is the backlog draining and some is 28 stages being slower than one merge, and these counts do not separate what the pipeline landed from what was landed by hand. A like-for-like throughput figure is not something we can honestly produce yet.

Not established. That everything goes through it. During this same week the team was also landing changes by hand, on a branch named for exactly that, while the pipeline was repaired underneath them. That is what a cutover week actually looks like, and it would be easy and dishonest to leave out.

Not finished. The item behind all of this has reached its final phase and its status still reads in-progress. There is more to do, and the repair list above is what has actually been closed.

What has been bought is narrower than "we automated our process." A change that goes through the gate meets the same 28 stages, in the same order, whether it arrives at 09:00 on a Monday or 15:00 on a Sunday. Nobody has the authority to wave one through — which also means nobody carries the quiet weight of being the person who could have.
