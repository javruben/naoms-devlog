Taking back a "done"

The feature: the terminal cockpit — the surface for driving NAOMS from a terminal — reaching the state where it could be called finished.

A completion marker in this project is not a mood. It is granted at the end of a defined sequence, and the last stage of that sequence is a review that is allowed to refuse.

Before: it was marked finished on the 18th, cleanly, with nothing outstanding. Now: it is not. The final review stage found drift, the finished marker was downgraded, and then removed entirely.

A cockpit is the surface you drive a system from. Marking one finished is a statement that somebody could sit down and run the product through it without hitting a wall, which is a heavier claim than it sounds.

A "done" you can take back is worth more than a "done" you cannot. The value of a completion marker is entirely in whether it is ever refused. One that is only ever granted is a formality with a status field attached.

There is a version of this week where none of it is published. The tests were green, the marker had been granted, and nobody outside would have known the difference for months.

What happened. Two separate reversals, a day apart. The first downgraded the finished state to conditional. The second removed it, citing fourteen pieces of follow-on work still open. Four regression rules were added the same day to catch the same drift again.

The second reversal is the one that took nerve, because the first had already absorbed the embarrassment. Downgrading to conditional would have been a defensible place to stop, and stopping there is how a conditional pass quietly becomes a pass.

Drift is the word for a gap that opens between what a thing claims and what it does, without either side changing dramatically. It is the failure mode that green tests are worst at catching, because nothing broke.

Why the follow-on count is the whole argument

Fourteen open pieces of follow-on work is not a rounding error against a finished feature. It is the difference between a thing that works and a thing that is being described as working.

The awkward part is that the tests were green. Nothing in the automated record objected, which is exactly the condition under which a finished marker gets granted and nobody looks again.

Conditional is a comfortable resting place precisely because it is defensible in both directions. It admits a problem and keeps the claim, and almost nothing ever leaves it.

A review stage that can only agree is decoration. The cost of one that can disagree is weeks like this one, where a public claim gets withdrawn and the withdrawal is the news.

The four regression rules added the same day are the part that makes the episode useful rather than merely honest. A lesson recorded in prose is forgotten; a lesson recorded as a rule that fails a run is not.

The name you assumed was canonical

The feature: the reliability of every read that looks something up by its type — which is most of them.

Before: a recorded event of one type could arrive in the queryable graph labelled as something else, and nothing said so. Now: the mapping is being made deterministic, with rules that fail a run when it is not.

A test written against the name you expected proves your expectation. It does not prove the system produces that name. When those two differ, the test passes and production quietly reads the wrong thing.

The queryable graph is a view built from the record of signed events. Building it means turning each event into a node, and turning a thing into another thing is where names get chosen.

What the trap looks like. A signed event of type device.approved is turned into a node in the graph. The node's type is not guaranteed to be device.approved — a generic conversion step can label it operator_event, or another alias entirely.

Everything downstream that looks for the original name finds nothing, and finds nothing silently. There is no error, because from the system's point of view nothing went wrong: a node exists, it simply has a different label than the one being asked for.

The generic step is doing something reasonable. It converts events of many types through one path, and a single path needs a single naming policy, so it picks one. The bug is not in the choice; it is in the choice being invisible to everything that later asks by name.

Why it kept happening

This had bitten the project so many times that it earned a dedicated review of the literature this week, rather than a fix and a shrug.

The reason it recurs is that every individual instance looks like a typo. Each one is found, corrected, and closed, and the general shape — a generic conversion step choosing a name — survives to produce the next one.

The response this week was deterministic conversion plus new checker rules, so a mismatch between the recorded name and the graph name fails a run rather than waiting for a person to notice. Making the mapping deterministic is the fix; gating it is what makes the fix stay fixed.

What is not finished is the sweep. Deterministic conversion and the rules that enforce it are the mechanism; every existing read that assumed a name has not been re-checked against it, and that is where the remaining instances live.

What this means, in plain terms

Most of what lands in a week belongs to the thing it landed in. A few things generalise past it. These five are the ones this week actually paid for — and this week they were paid for expensively.

A completion marker that is never refused is not a marker

The terminal cockpit was marked finished on the 18th and un-marked on the 19th, when the final review stage found drift behind green tests and named fourteen open follow-ons. The withdrawal is what gives every other completion marker its meaning. A status that can only be granted is a label, not a check.

A name you assume is canonical may be an alias

An event recorded as one type can arrive in the queryable graph under a different label, so tests written against the expected name pass while production silently reads the wrong thing. Make the mapping deterministic and gate it, because a green test cannot see a name it was never told to doubt.

When a large mechanical change goes wrong, restart it

The 603-file move was rolled back to a known-good base, the automated rewrite's exclusion rules were corrected, and the whole move was re-landed under review rather than patched forward. Patching forward would have left the original mistake buried under its own corrections. A bad bulk edit is cheaper to redo than to repair.

A metric you optimise directly stops measuring

Wednesday was the lightest day of the week and it was a consolidation day: an onboarding loop wired up as a test, the first slice of a docs-first search, two-device scaffolding, one more guard. None of it photographs well and all of it is load-bearing. A count is a pulse, not a score — pad it and you are lying to yourself with your own instrument.

The two directions a plan can drift are not equal

A plan can say finished when the code is not, or say not-started when the work is already running and tested. The first misleads everyone relying on you; the second costs bookkeeping. Prefer the cheap drift, and fix it by deriving status from reality rather than maintaining it beside reality.

What would prove this week's honesty claims wrong

Five things this report claims are only half-proven. Each is stated as a commitment, and each names the evidence that would settle it.

  • The withdrawn completion marker has not been re-earned. Fourteen pieces of follow-on work were open when it was taken back. Re-granting it before those close would make the withdrawal a gesture rather than a check.
  • The new guard rules are advisory, and advisory is not a gate. They report and they do not block. Until they block, they measure the problem rather than prevent it.
  • The first tier of daemon signing is broken and known to be. Publishing the finding alongside the capability is the right disclosure and is not a fix.
  • A reopened performance regression is still open. It was reopened honestly rather than closed quietly, which improves the record and not the software.
  • The relocated test files re-landed under review, not under proof. A move that was rolled back once and corrected is a move that deserves a run proving nothing was lost, and that run is not reported here.
